## What even is food science?

**MAYA:** So this is Lesson 1: Food Science and the Canadian Food System. And we start with the definition. **Food science studies the production, processing, preparing, evaluating, and use of food.**

**DEV:** That's… everything? That's just "food" with extra steps.

**MAYA:** It's everything from the farm gate to your plate. And the food industry's whole job across that journey is to keep food three things — say it with me, because this trio comes back all course long: **safe, nutritious, and tasty.**

**DEV:** Safe, nutritious, tasty. The holy trinity.

**MAYA:** Exam radar, by the way — 🎯 — you should know the **sub-fields of food science** and be able to match each one to its description. There are five:

1. **Food Chemistry** — the structure, composition, and properties of foods. That's Lessons 2 and 3.
2. **Food Analysis** — the methods used to understand food structure and composition. Also Lesson 2.
3. **Food Microbiology** — the roles of microorganisms in foodborne illness *and* fermentation. Lessons 5 and 12.
4. **Food Processing** — manufacturing, packaging, and handling of foods. That's the whole middle of the course, Lessons 6 through 11.
5. **Food Engineering** — thermodynamics and energy transfer during processing. We only touch this briefly.

**DEV:** So microbiology is both the villain *and* the hero? Foodborne illness and fermentation?

**MAYA:** Exactly — same tiny organisms, totally different outcomes depending on whether you're controlling them. That tension is basically the plot of this entire course.

**DEV:** Microbes: a redemption arc.

## The canning guy with a statue

**MAYA:** Little history. Outside the headquarters of the **Institute of Food Technologists** — the IFT, the big international food science organization — there's a statue of a man named **François Nicolas Appert**.

**DEV:** What did he do to earn statue status?

**MAYA:** He invented **canning**. Napoleon-era France offered a prize for a way to preserve food for armies on the move, and Appert figured out that if you seal food in a container and heat it, it keeps. And here's the kicker — this was *before* anyone knew germs existed. He didn't know *why* it worked.

**DEV:** He speedran food preservation without a theory of microbiology?

**MAYA:** People preserved, fermented, and dried food for ten thousand years by trial and error before the science caught up. Exam radar 🎯: **Appert, statue at IFT headquarters, invention of canning.** That's a known multiple-choice question, and the wrong answers will try to lure you with "an ancient Egyptian" and "winemaking." Don't fall for it.

**DEV:** Appert. Canning. Got it. What's the IFT to us, besides statue owners?

**MAYA:** They set the education standards for food science degrees — 55 essential learning outcomes. UBC's Food Science program is one of the few in Canada approved by IFT, and FNH 200 introduces a slice of those outcomes: food chemistry, microbiology, safety, sensory science, quality assurance, food law. They also publish a yearly **Top Ten Food Trends** list, because even scientists like a listicle.

## Canada: a very big farm with very specific regions

**DEV:** Okay, "Canadian Food System." What makes ours special?

**MAYA:** Scale and diversity. Canada has a **vast and diverse food system**, and the food industry has a **strong impact on Canada's economy** — those exact phrases are the takeaway. But the testable detail is *where* things grow. Exam radar 🎯: **Which provinces are the major producers of cereal grains?**

**DEV:** Grains… I want to say the prairies? The flat middle bit?

**MAYA:** The flat middle bit is correct: **Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba**. The classic trick answer is "Saskatchewan only" — but it's all three prairie provinces. BC and Ontario are not the grain giants.

**DEV:** So what does BC do?

**MAYA:** Fruit, among other things — which brings us back to my apple.

## The eight-month-old apple

**MAYA:** Apples in BC are typically harvested in **October**. One month of harvest, twelve months of apples in stores. How?

**DEV:** Fridges. Big fridges.

**MAYA:** Better: **controlled atmosphere storage**. Giant sealed rooms where we control — and this is an exam radar 🎯 "all of the above" special — **temperature, humidity, oxygen, AND carbon dioxide.**

**DEV:** Wait, why mess with the oxygen?

**MAYA:** Because the apple is *alive*. It's still breathing — respiring, ripening, aging. Normal air is about **21% oxygen** — memorize that number, it's a quiz question. Drop the oxygen way down, chill the room, bump the CO₂, and the apple's metabolism slows to a crawl. It basically hibernates.

**DEV:** We put apples in cryosleep.

**MAYA:** We put apples in cryosleep. And when the room opens months later, they wake up crisp. That's why your June apple was picked in October and is still good.

## One apple, five products

**DEV:** The lecture had this activity — ranking apple products by processing complexity. Apple, juice, sauce, cider…

**MAYA:** Right, and it's a great mental model. A **fresh apple** is minimally processed — washed, waxed, stored. **Apple sauce**: chopped, cooked, packed. **Apple juice**: pressed, filtered, pasteurized. And **apple cider** — the hard stuff — adds a whole biological step. Exam radar 🎯: in cider production, **yeast** is added to apple juice and produces **ethanol**. Yeast, ethanol. Not bacteria, not acetic acid — that combo makes vinegar, which is a different day's problem.

**DEV:** And the imported apple from New Zealand slots in there too?

**MAYA:** Yes — distance adds its own complexity. A US or New Zealand apple needs the whole cold chain: storage, shipping, logistics, all while keeping it safe, nutritious, and tasty. Processing complexity isn't just what you do to the food — it's everything required to get it to your plate in good shape.

**DEV:** Okay, but pressing juice must leave a mountain of apple mush. Do we just landfill that?

**MAYA:** Great instinct — that's the waste question, and it's exam radar 🎯. The pressed-out pulp, the pomace, becomes a **value-added product: pectin.**

**DEV:** Pectin like… the jam thickener?

**MAYA:** The very same. The gelling agent that sets your jams and jellies is extracted from fruit-juice waste. Industry sells its garbage as an ingredient. It's the circle of life, but for apples.

## We vote with our forks

**DEV:** The lecture also had all these Statistics Canada consumption charts. What's the takeaway — do I need the numbers?

**MAYA:** The takeaway is the pattern: **consumers and consumer demand strongly influence food consumption trends.** What Canada produces and stocks shifts year over year — some categories rise, others fall — fresh produce, meats, dairy, wheat flour, alcohol all move with demand. Look at a decade of data and you'll see things like beef and pork declining while other categories climb. The system isn't static; it chases us.

**DEV:** So when everyone suddenly wants oat milk, the food system pivots.

**MAYA:** Exactly. Demand upstream reshapes farms, factories, and shelves downstream. And one sobering closing image from the lecture: bins of wasted food. We built this astonishing system — and then a huge share of what it produces gets thrown away. Keep that in the back of your mind for the whole course.
