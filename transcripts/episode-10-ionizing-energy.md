## Where irradiation sits on the spectrum

**MAYA:** Start with physics. The electromagnetic spectrum runs from long radio waves down to tiny gamma rays. Two zones matter for us 🎯:

- **Infrared** — that's your **stove element**. The known question: what type of energy is used in everyday cooking? **Infrared.** Not microwave, not UV.
- **Gamma rays and X-rays** — short wavelength, high energy. This is **ionizing energy**, and the reason we use it is **good penetration power** — it goes right through packaged food.

**DEV:** So irradiation is just a much shorter wavelength than my stove.

**MAYA:** Same spectrum, different end. Three approved sources 🎯:

1. **Gamma radiation** from **Cobalt-60** or **Cesium-137**
2. **X-rays** from a machine source at or below **5 MeV**
3. **Electrons** from a machine source at or below **10 MeV**

And the unit of dose is the **Gray** (or kiloGray, kGy) — **the energy absorbed by the food**. Canada's ceiling: **under 10 kGy**.

**DEV:** Cobalt-60 sounds alarming. What does the facility look like?

**MAYA:** Cobalt-60 rods live at the bottom of a **water pool**; food on a conveyor passes above, absorbs the gamma rays, and moves on. The source never touches the food, and the food never touches the source. Note the real regulatory worry here 🎯 — it's not the food, it's **the safety of the personnel** operating the plant.

## Two ways it kills things

**MAYA:** How does the energy actually preserve food? Two mechanisms 🎯 — this distinction is a guaranteed question.

**Direct effect → damage to DNA.** The radiation smashes the genetic material of living things. Consequences, and this one is an emphatic **"All!"**: **destruction of microorganisms, destruction of insects, inactivation of parasites, delaying of ripening, sprouting inhibition, and destruction of enzymes.**

**DEV:** Delaying ripening and stopping sprouting — because you've damaged the DNA of the *food itself*, which is still alive.

**MAYA:** Exactly why irradiated potatoes don't sprout eyes. That's the same living-tissue idea as the controlled-atmosphere apples.

**Indirect effect → free radicals.** The radiation splits water molecules, producing **free radicals** and **hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)**, which then attack microbial cells chemically. Also formed: **radiolytic products**. The exam wording 🎯: irradiation preserves indirectly by forming **free radicals, hydrogen peroxide, and radiolytic products** — but **not** "radiative food." Food does not become radioactive. Hold that thought.

## The three -ations

**MAYA:** Now the vocabulary that Judy specifically flags as "terminologies" 🎯. Three dose levels, three targets — and yes, they sound almost identical on purpose:

- **Radicidation** — **under 10 kGy** — kills **disease-causing** (pathogenic) microorganisms. Mnemonic: radi-**CID**-ation, homicide, the dangerous ones.
- **Radurization** — **under 10 kGy** — kills **spoilage-causing** microorganisms. Radu-**DUR**-ization extends *durability*.
- **Radappertization** — **over 10 kGy** — full **sterilization**. Named after **Appert**, the canning guy with the statue from Episode 1.

**DEV:** Appert gets a statue *and* a radiation process named after him. Overachiever.

**MAYA:** And notice the parallel to Lesson 6: radicidation ≈ pasteurization, radappertization ≈ commercial sterilization. Same three-tier logic, different energy source.

## D-value returns, with new units

**MAYA:** Speaking of which — the **D-value** comes back 🎯, redefined for radiation: **the absorbed energy dose that produces a decimal reduction (90% kill) of the target microorganism.** Same concept as Lesson 6, but measured in **kGy instead of minutes**.

And the pH rule carries over unchanged: **acid foods 5D, low-acid foods 12D.**

**DEV:** So there's a calculation lurking.

**MAYA:** And it's a great one 🎯. A manufacturer wants to irradiate FoodABC; the target organism's **D-value is 0.9 kGy**. Will it be approved?

**DEV:** Okay… if it's low-acid, 12D means 12 × 0.9 = **10.8 kGy**. That's over Canada's 10 kGy limit. But if it's acidic, 5D is 4.5 kGy, which is fine.

**MAYA:** So the answer is **"it depends on the pH of the product."** Not yes, not no. That single question ties together the dose limit, the D-value, and pH 4.6 — it's the most FNH-200 question ever written.

## Is it safe? The two public fears

**MAYA:** Two concerns, two answers 🎯:

**"Does the food become radioactive?"** **No.** The energy levels are too low — **under 10 MeV** — to induce radioactivity in the food. Low exposure, no residual radiation. The food is not radioactive when it leaves and it isn't when you eat it.

**"What about weird by-products?"** These are the **unique radiolytic products** — compounds formed by the radiation. The scientific consensus reflected in the lecture: they occur in tiny amounts, and many are the same compounds produced by ordinary cooking.

And to minimize damage further, three **precautionary steps** 🎯: **irradiate frozen foods** (less free water means fewer free radicals), **irradiate under vacuum** (no oxygen for oxidation), and add **free radical scavengers** — antioxidants like vitamins C and E, mopping up radicals before they do harm.

**DEV:** Freeze it, de-oxygenate it, and give it antioxidants. Belt and braces again.

## Canada's short list and the radura

**MAYA:** Regulation. Two agencies, and the exam separates their jobs 🎯:

- **CFIA** — responsible for the **labelling and advertising** of irradiated foods, under **Division 16** of the Food and Drug Regulations. (Health Canada writes the regulations; same division as food additives.)
- **Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission** — **regulates the uses** of the radiation sources.
- **Atomic Energy of Canada Limited** — **develops the irradiators**. It's not a food-labelling body, and "Canadian Irradiation Centre" isn't the answer either.

**Approved irradiated foods in Canada** — a famously short list 🎯: **potatoes, onions, wheat and wheat flour, dried spices, and ground beef (approved in 2017).** Five. The US has approved a far longer list — the Canada-vs-US contrast is an explicit learning objective.

**DEV:** Potatoes and onions to stop sprouting, spices because they're full of microbes, ground beef because *E. coli*.

**MAYA:** Each one solves a specific problem. Now labelling 🎯: irradiated food must carry a statement — **"treated by irradiation," "treated with radiation," or "irradiated"** — plus the **radura symbol**, that green stylized plant in a broken circle.

The wrinkle: **if an irradiated food is an ingredient in another food, it must be declared only if it makes up 10% or more of the final product.**

**DEV:** So the exam question about a frozen turkey dinner with irradiated spices at 1% of the weight…

**MAYA:** …requires **no irradiation declaration at all**, because 1% is below the 10% threshold. And note there's no "hazard symbol" in Canadian irradiation labelling — that option is pure invention.

**MAYA:** Last fact 🎯: where are Canada's irradiated-food processing facilities? **Port Coquitlam, BC and Laval, Quebec.** Not on any UBC campus — the question dangles three UBC options to bait you.

**DEV:** So after all that — is it safe?

**MAYA:** The lecture ends on "What's your take?" and asks you to articulate your own values. The science says: not radioactive, dose-limited, labelled, and approved for five foods. What you do with that is genuinely up to you — but now your feeling has facts attached.
