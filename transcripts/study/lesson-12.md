| 1 | Define toxicity, hazard, and risk as used in FNH 200. | Toxicity = how "bad" or toxic a substance intrinsically is; hazard = the probability of contacting something toxic. Risk = how likely a hazard will actually happen, plus the impact if it does. |
| 2 | Give the course example of something with very high toxicity but low risk, including its LD50. | Botulinal toxin is among the most toxic substances known, with an estimated human LD50 of about 1 ng/kg body weight. Risk stays low because the hazard (probability of contact) is very small. |
| 3 | What are the four regions of the dose-response curve (recalled from Lesson 4)? | No effect, then a threshold, then the dose range of increasing response, then maximum effect. The dose makes the poison. |
| 4 | In the "Perceived Importance of Food Components" table, how do popular notion and regulatory concern rank hazards? | They run in opposite directions: popular notion is Great for food additives and Minimal for microbiological contaminants, while regulatory concern is Minimal for additives and Great for microbiological contaminants. Order listed: food additives, agricultural chemicals, natural toxicants, environmental contaminants, nutritional properties, microbiological contaminants. |
| 5 | What are the three categories in the Lesson 12 toxicant classification table? | (1) Naturally occurring toxicants - constituents (part of the food itself); (2) naturally occurring toxicants - contaminants (produced by something living on or in the food); (3) environmental toxicants (introduced from outside). |
| 6 | List the six classes of naturally occurring toxicant CONSTITUENTS with the lecture's example for each. | Cholinesterase inhibitors - solanine in potatoes; cyanogenic glycosides - amygdalin in apple seeds; glucosinolates - sinigrin in cabbage. Protease inhibitors - in soy beans; nitrites - in green leafy plants; allergens - beta-lactoglobulin in milk, peanut proteins, soy proteins. |
| 7 | What are the three groups of naturally occurring toxicant CONTAMINANTS? | Mycotoxins (from moulds), bacterial toxins, and seafood toxins. All are made by an organism living on or in the food rather than being part of the food itself. |
| 8 | Name the five mycotoxins listed in the lecture and the two food examples given. | Aflatoxin, patulin, ochratoxin A, vomitoxin, and zearalenone. Examples: aflatoxin in mouldy peanuts and vomitoxin in wheat. |
| 9 | Name the two bacterial toxins and the four seafood toxins listed as natural contaminants. | Bacterial toxins: botulinum toxin and staphylococcal toxin. Seafood toxins: histamine, saxitoxin, domoic acid, and tetrodotoxin. |
| 10 | List the five categories of ENVIRONMENTAL toxicants with the lecture's examples. | Food packaging residues (monomers of plastic resins); pesticide residues - herbicides, insecticides, fungicides (e.g. malathion residues on fruit); heavy metals - lead, mercury, cadmium (e.g. lead in vegetables, mercury in large ocean fish such as swordfish). Animal drugs - antibiotics and hormones (e.g. penicillin in milk); radioisotopes - from soil or from radioactive fallout. |
| 11 | How does the lecture group the SOURCES of environmental toxicants? | Industrial activity (mercury, lead, dioxins, polychlorinated biphenyls, radioisotopes); agricultural activity (pesticide and herbicide residues, antibiotic residues); food processing (packaging residues, cleaning supplies, equipment maintenance); naturally occurring environmental toxicants (mercury, radioisotopes). |
| 12 | Allergens such as nuts are classified as what, and toxins found on mouldy nuts are classified as what? | Allergens such as nuts are natural CONSTITUENTS, because the protein is part of the food itself. Toxins on mouldy nuts (e.g. aflatoxin) are natural CONTAMINANTS, made by a mould growing on the food. |
| 13 | The meltdown of the Fukushima Daiichi power facility after the March 2011 tsunami introduced which type of toxicant into the food chain? | Environmental toxicants - radioisotopes from radioactive fallout. Not bacterial, not constituent, not contaminant. |
| 14 | What are the normal and green-part solanine levels in potatoes? | Normal level is 2 to 13 mg per 100 g of potato. The green part contains 80 to 100 mg. |
| 15 | Solanine: toxicant class, effect of cooking, symptoms, and how to minimize risk? | It is a cholinesterase inhibitor and a naturally occurring CONSTITUENT of potatoes, and it is heat resistant, so cooking will not destroy it; symptoms are drowsiness, vomiting and diarrhea, sensitivity, difficulty in breathing, and death in extreme cases. Minimize risk through farming and storage practices (e.g. keeping potatoes dark so they do not green). |
| 16 | Name the toxin naturally found in (bitter) almonds, and the related Health Canada warning. | Amygdalin, a cyanogenic glycoside. Health Canada has issued warnings about the risks of consuming apricot kernels because of cyanide poisoning. |
| 17 | Name the neurotoxin first discovered on Prince Edward Island, where severely affected individuals suffered memory loss - and what do the other three seafood toxins cause? | Domoic acid (amnesic shellfish poisoning). Saxitoxin causes paralytic shellfish poisoning, tetrodotoxin is the pufferfish toxin, and histamine causes scombroid poisoning; BC tracks closures with the BCCDC Shellfish Harvesting Status Map. |
| 18 | "We can trim off the tiny mould grown on the food." This is true, but it depends on what? | All three of: storage temperature, texture of the food, and size of the mould grown. A small spot on hard food can be trimmed generously, but soft, moist food must be discarded because the mould threads and toxins have already spread. |
| 19 | What are the five sources of foodborne illnesses listed in the lecture? | Microorganisms, parasites, plants, animals, and chemicals. |
| 20 | "A situation in which two or more people experience a similar illness after ingestion of the same food and where epidemiological evidence implicates the food as the source of the illness" defines what? | An outbreak - not an incident and not a single case. The threshold is two or more people plus epidemiological evidence implicating the food. |
| 21 | Give the details of the Maple Leaf Foods 2008 outbreak. | Listeriosis traced to cold cuts (ready-to-eat deli meats). There were 57 confirmed cases and 22 deaths. |
| 22 | Distinguish food intoxication from food infection, and classify the lecture's organisms. | Intoxication = the toxin is already present in the food before you eat it (Clostridium botulinum; Escherichia coli O157:H7 Shiga toxin). Infection = the living organism is swallowed and multiplies in you (Salmonella spp.; Listeria monocytogenes); hepatitis A and norovirus are listed separately as viruses. |
| 23 | Clostridium botulinum: symptoms, onset time, and common foods? | Dizziness, double vision, difficult swallowing, respiratory or cardiac paralysis, death - 12 to 36 hours. Foods: low-acid foods in anaerobic conditions, improperly home-canned, vacuum-packed and poorly refrigerated foods, and baked potatoes wrapped in foil; spores are detected in carrots and potatoes (soil contamination), fresh and processed meats, and honey and corn syrup. |
| 24 | Clostridium botulinum: prevention measures? | Commercially sterile products must receive a "botulinum cook" (12 D); use sodium nitrite in cured meats; properly refrigerate vegetable and fruit juice. Honey, corn syrup and molasses must not be fed to infants under 1 year of age, because pasteurization of honey does NOT inactivate the spores. |
| 25 | Escherichia coli O157:H7 (Shiga toxin): symptoms, onset time, and common foods? | Bloody diarrhea, kidney failure, and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) in severe cases - 12 to 72 hours. Foods: undercooked ground meat, raw milk, unpasteurized apple juice/cider, lettuce, spinach, alfalfa sprouts, and contaminated water supply (the Walkerton, Ontario outbreak). |
| 26 | Escherichia coli O157:H7: prevention, and its two survival quirks? | Practice good food sanitation and hand washing; it is killed by normal cooking, pasteurization, and ionizing radiation. However, it survives acidic conditions (hence unpasteurized apple juice cases) and does not grow well at temperatures below 8 degrees C. |
| 27 | Salmonella spp.: symptoms, onset time, common foods, and the retail chicken statistic? | Nausea, fever, vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhea - 6 to 48 hours; foods are raw meats and poultry, eggs, milk and dairy products. About 60% of chickens sold in the retail market are contaminated with Salmonella. |
| 28 | Salmonella: why is frozen-and-thawed poultry no safer than fresh poultry? | Salmonella is easily killed by heat (normal cooking and pasteurization) and by ionizing energy, but it survives freezing and dehydration. Consequently, frozen and thawed poultry can be just as contaminated as fresh poultry. |
| 29 | Listeria monocytogenes: symptoms, onset time, common foods, and its two dangerous traits? | Flu-like symptoms, meningitis, septicemia or pneumonia, birth defects, still birth - 1 day to 3 weeks; foods are raw milk, dairy products, vegetables, fish and meat products. It grows slowly on foods during refrigerated storage and is fairly tolerant to salt, so refrigerate raw materials and high-risk foods below 4 degrees C (it is easily killed by cooking and pasteurization). |
| 30 | The two foodborne viruses: symptoms, onset, foods, and prevention? | Hepatitis A - fever, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fatigue, swelling of the liver, jaundice (10 to 50 days); foods prepared with human contact and contaminated water. Norovirus - vomiting, diarrhea, headache, fever (24 to 48 hours); sewage, contaminated water, contaminated salad ingredients, raw seafood, infected food workers; prevention for both is hand washing and good personal hygiene, potable water, and avoiding raw seafood / cooking all seafood. |
| 31 | List the WHO and BC Health Files ten rules for safe food handling. | Choose foods processed for safety (e.g. pasteurized over raw); cook foods thoroughly; eat cooked foods immediately; store food promptly and carefully; reheat cooked foods thoroughly. Avoid cross-contamination between raw and cooked food; wash hands repeatedly; keep all kitchen surfaces clean; do not let anyone with diarrhea or infected sores prepare food; wash all fruits and vegetables - and if in doubt, throw it out. |
| 32 | What is the Temperature Danger Zone (TDZ), and why does it matter? | The TDZ is 4 degrees C to 60 degrees C (40 to 140 degrees F). Bacteria can grow and/or produce toxins in food left in the TDZ, so keep cold food below 4 degrees C and hot food above 60 degrees C. |
| 33 | What does HACCP stand for, who created it, and what are its seven steps? | Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point, created by NASA to ensure astronauts' foods are safe. Steps: 1 identify hazards; 2 determine the critical control points (CCPs); 3 establish control measures (critical limits or thresholds); 4 establish procedures to monitor CCPs; 5 establish corrective actions; 6 keep records; 7 verify procedures. |
| 34 | Work through the HACCP example for a canned food. | Hazard = C. botulinum spores; CCP = canning; control measures = temperature and time; monitoring = thermometer at the cold spot plus data logging. Corrective action = calculate the additional time needed, then keep records and verify procedures. |
| 35 | What are the recognized hazard categories used in HACCP? | Allergenic, biological, chemical, and physical. "Environmental" is NOT one of the standard HACCP hazard categories. |

## Practice MCQs (instructor style)

# FNH 200 — Lesson 12 Practice MCQs (Toxicants in Foods and Foodborne Illnesses)

### Q1. Which of the following pairs a term with its correct FNH 200 definition?
A. Toxicity — the probability of contacting something toxic
B. Hazard — how likely an event is to happen, plus its impact
C. Risk — how "bad" or toxic a substance intrinsically is
D. Hazard — the probability of contacting something toxic
E. None of the above

**Answer: D** — Toxicity is how toxic a substance intrinsically is, hazard is the probability of contact, and risk is how likely the hazard will happen plus the impact. A, B and C each shift the definition onto the wrong term.

### Q2. According to the "Perceived Importance of Food Components" table, which statement is correct?
A. Both the public and regulators rank food additives as the greatest hazard
B. Popular notion ranks microbiological contaminants greatest; regulatory concern ranks food additives greatest
C. Popular notion ranks food additives greatest and microbiological contaminants minimal; regulatory concern is exactly the reverse
D. Both the public and regulators rank microbiological contaminants as minimal
E. All of the above

**Answer: C** — The table's two arrows point in opposite directions: popular notion runs Great (additives) to Minimal (microbiological), while regulatory concern runs Minimal (additives) to Great (microbiological).

### Q3. Peanuts can be linked to toxicants from more than one category. Which pairing is correct?
A. Peanut allergen protein = natural contaminant; aflatoxin on mouldy peanuts = natural constituent
B. Peanut allergen protein = natural constituent; aflatoxin on mouldy peanuts = natural contaminant
C. Peanut allergen protein = environmental toxicant; aflatoxin on mouldy peanuts = natural constituent
D. Both are environmental toxicants
E. None of the above

**Answer: B** — The allergenic protein is part of the food itself, so it is a constituent, whereas aflatoxin is produced by a mould growing on the food, making it a natural contaminant.

### Q4. Which of the following are ENVIRONMENTAL toxicants?
(i) Penicillin residues in milk
(ii) Mercury in large ocean fish such as swordfish
(iii) Solanine in green potatoes
(iv) Radioisotopes from radioactive fallout
(v) Vomitoxin in wheat

A. (i), (iii)
B. (ii), (iv)
C. (i), (ii), (iv)
D. (ii), (iv), (v)
E. (i), (ii), (iii), (iv), (v)

**Answer: C** — Animal drugs, heavy metals and radioisotopes are environmental toxicants; solanine is a natural constituent and vomitoxin is a mycotoxin, i.e. a natural contaminant.

### Q5. The meltdown of the Fukushima Daiichi Power Facility after the tsunami in Japan in March 2011 introduces this type of toxicant into the food chain:
A. Bacterial
B. Constituent
C. Contaminant
D. Environmental
E. Packaging residues

**Answer: D** — Radioactive fallout is an environmental toxicant (radioisotopes), introduced into the food chain from outside the food rather than produced in or by it.

### Q6. "We can trim off the tiny mould grown on the food." It is true, and depends on...
(i) Storage temperature
(ii) Texture of the food
(iii) Size of the mould grown

A. (i)
B. (ii)
C. (i) and (ii)
D. (ii) and (iii)
E. (i), (ii), and (iii)

**Answer: E** — All three factors matter; in a soft, moist food the mould threads and their toxins have already spread beyond the visible spot, so it must be discarded.

### Q7. Which statement about solanine in potatoes is correct?
A. Normal level is 2–13 mg per 100 g; the green part contains 80–100 mg; it is heat resistant
B. Normal level is 80–100 mg per 100 g; the green part contains 2–13 mg; it is heat resistant
C. Normal level is 2–13 mg per 100 g; the green part contains 80–100 mg; it is destroyed by boiling
D. Normal level is 2–13 mg per 100 g; the green part contains 20–30 mg; it is heat labile
E. None of the above

**Answer: A** — Green parts carry roughly ten times the normal solanine level, and because solanine is heat resistant, cooking does not make a green potato safe; control depends on farming and storage practices.

### Q8. Name the toxin naturally found in almonds.
A. Cholinesterase inhibitors
B. Amygdalin, cyanogenic glycoside
C. Protease inhibitors
D. Nitrates
E. Allergens

**Answer: B** — Amygdalin is a cyanogenic glycoside, the same toxin behind Health Canada's warning about cyanide poisoning from apricot kernels.

### Q9. Name the neurotoxin that was first discovered on Prince Edward Island, where severely affected individuals suffered from memory loss.
A. Histamine
B. Saxitoxin
C. Domoic acid
D. Tetrodotoxin
E. Ochratoxin A

**Answer: C** — Domoic acid causes amnesic shellfish poisoning; saxitoxin causes paralytic shellfish poisoning, tetrodotoxin is the pufferfish toxin, and histamine causes scombroid poisoning.

### Q10. "A situation in which two or more people experience a similar illness after ingestion of the same food and where epidemiological evidence implicates the food as the source of the illness" is called:
A. An incident
B. A single case
C. An outbreak
D. A recall
E. None of the above

**Answer: C** — Two or more people plus epidemiological evidence implicating the food defines an outbreak; the Maple Leaf Foods 2008 listeriosis outbreak in cold cuts had 57 confirmed cases and 22 deaths.

### Q11. Which of the following are classified as food INTOXICATIONS rather than food infections?
(i) Clostridium botulinum
(ii) Salmonella spp.
(iii) Escherichia coli O157:H7 (Shiga toxin)
(iv) Listeria monocytogenes
(v) Norovirus

A. (i), (ii)
B. (i), (iii)
C. (ii), (iv)
D. (i), (iii), (v)
E. (i), (ii), (iii), (iv)

**Answer: B** — In an intoxication the toxin is already present in the food; Salmonella and Listeria are infections (the live organism multiplies in you) and norovirus is listed separately as a virus.

### Q12. Which organism is matched with the correct symptom onset time?
A. Listeria monocytogenes — 6 to 48 hours
B. Salmonella spp. — 1 day to 3 weeks
C. Hepatitis A — 24 to 48 hours
D. Clostridium botulinum — 12 to 36 hours
E. Norovirus — 10 to 50 days

**Answer: D** — Botulism onset is 12–36 hours. The correct values for the others are Listeria 1 day–3 weeks, Salmonella 6–48 hours, hepatitis A 10–50 days, and norovirus 24–48 hours.

### Q13. Which of the following statements about Salmonella spp. is/are true?
A. About 60% of chickens sold in the retail market are contaminated with Salmonella
B. It is easily killed by normal cooking, pasteurization and ionizing energy
C. It survives freezing and dehydration
D. Frozen and thawed poultry can be just as contaminated as fresh poultry
E. All of the above

**Answer: E** — Salmonella is heat-sensitive but freeze- and drying-tolerant, which is why freezing is not a kill step and thawed poultry must be handled exactly like fresh poultry.

### Q14. Which of the following are recognized HACCP hazard categories?
(i) Allergenic
(ii) Biological
(iii) Chemical
(iv) Environmental
(v) Physical

A. (ii), (iii)
B. (i), (ii), (iii)
C. (ii), (iii), (v)
D. (i), (ii), (iii), (v)
E. (i), (ii), (iii), (iv), (v)

**Answer: D** — The recognized categories are allergenic, biological, chemical and physical; "environmental" describes a toxicant source, not a HACCP hazard category.

### Q15. Which of the following statements is FALSE?
A. Honey, corn syrup and molasses must not be fed to infants under 1 year of age
B. Pasteurization of honey inactivates Clostridium botulinum spores
C. The Temperature Danger Zone is 4 °C to 60 °C (40–140 °F)
D. Sodium nitrite is used in cured meats to control Clostridium botulinum
E. Commercially sterile canned foods must receive a "botulinum cook" (12 D)

**Answer: B** — Pasteurization of honey does NOT inactivate the spores, which is exactly why honey is prohibited for infants under one year of age.
