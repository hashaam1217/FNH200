# Lesson 10: Ionizing Energy — Study Deck

## Flashcards

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What type of energy shown on the electromagnetic spectrum is used in our everyday cooking (e.g. a stove element)? | Infrared. It is not microwave, ultraviolet, X-ray or gamma-ray. |
| 2 | Where on the electromagnetic spectrum does food irradiation sit, and why is that region used? | At the short-wavelength, high-energy end: gamma-rays and X-rays (ionizing energy). It is used because of its good penetration power through food and packaging. |
| 3 | Name the three approved sources of ionizing energy for food irradiation. | 1) Gamma radiation from Cobalt-60 or Cesium-137; 2) X-rays generated from a machine source at or below 5 MeV; 3) Electrons generated from a machine source at or below 10 MeV. |
| 4 | What does the Gray (Gy; also kiloGray, kGy, or Rad) measure? | The energy absorbed by a food, i.e. the irradiation dose. |
| 5 | What is the maximum irradiation dose permitted in Canada? | Less than 10 kGy. |
| 6 | Describe the layout of a Cobalt-60 food irradiation facility. | Cobalt-60 rods sit at the bottom of a water pool; food on an overhead conveyor passes above the source, absorbs the gamma rays, and moves on. The source never touches the food. |
| 7 | What is the main regulatory concern in operating a food irradiation facility? | The safety of the personnel operating the plant, not the safety of the food. |
| 8 | What are the two mechanisms by which ionizing energy preserves food? | Direct effect: damage to DNA materials. Indirect effect: formation of free radicals and hydrogen peroxide from water. |
| 9 | List the six consequences of the DIRECT DNA damage caused by ionizing energy. | (i) Destruction of microorganisms, (ii) destruction of insects, (iii) inactivation of parasites, (iv) delaying of ripening, (v) sprouting inhibition, (vi) destruction of enzymes. The answer is All of them. |
| 10 | Why do irradiated potatoes and onions not sprout? | The direct DNA damage hits the still-living food tissue, giving sprouting inhibition and delayed ripening. |
| 11 | Ionizing energy preserves food INDIRECTLY by forming what? | Free radicals, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and radiolytic products, which attack microbial cells chemically. |
| 12 | Is 'radiative food' (radioactive food) formed by the indirect effect of irradiation? | No. Free radicals, hydrogen peroxide and radiolytic products are formed, but the food does not become radioactive. |
| 13 | Radicidation: what dose and what target? | Less than 10 kGy; destroys disease-causing (pathogenic) microorganisms. |
| 14 | Radurization: what dose and what target? | Less than 10 kGy; destroys spoilage-causing microorganisms. |
| 15 | Radappertization: what dose and what target? | Greater than 10 kGy; achieves sterilization. Named after Appert, the father of canning. |
| 16 | Which irradiation terminology is analogous to pasteurization, and which to commercial sterilization? | Radicidation is analogous to pasteurization; radappertization is analogous to commercial sterilization. |
| 17 | Which of the three irradiation treatments could NOT be approved in Canada, and why? | Radappertization, because it requires more than 10 kGy and Canada's limit is less than 10 kGy. |
| 18 | Define the D-value as used for ionizing energy. | The absorbed energy dosage that produces a decimal reduction (90% reduction) of the targeted microbial population. It is expressed in kGy rather than minutes. |
| 19 | For irradiation, how many decimal reductions are required for acid foods versus low-acid foods? | Acid foods 5D; low-acid foods 12D. |
| 20 | A manufacturer wants to irradiate FoodABC; the target organism's D-value is 0.9 kGy. Will the process be approved? | It depends on the pH of the product. Acid food: 5D = 4.5 kGy (under the limit); low-acid food: 12D = 10.8 kGy (over Canada's 10 kGy limit). |
| 21 | Public concern: does irradiated food become radioactive? | No. Exposure is low and the energy is below 10 MeV, too low to induce radioactivity in the food. |
| 22 | Public concern: what are the by-products of irradiation called? | Unique radiolytic products. They occur in tiny amounts and many are the same compounds produced by ordinary cooking. |
| 23 | List the three precautionary steps used to minimize irradiation damage to food. | Irradiating frozen foods, irradiating in vacuum, and adding free radical scavengers. |
| 24 | Why irradiate food frozen, why under vacuum, and what are free radical scavengers? | Frozen: less free water means fewer free radicals. Vacuum: no oxygen for oxidation. Scavengers are antioxidants such as vitamins C and E that mop up free radicals. |
| 25 | Which agency is responsible for the labelling and advertising of irradiated foods in Canada? | The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA), under Division 16 of the Food and Drug Regulations. |
| 26 | What is the role of the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission in food irradiation? | It regulates the uses of the radiation sources. |
| 27 | What is the role of Atomic Energy of Canada Limited (AECL) in food irradiation? | It develops the irradiators. It is not the labelling agency. |
| 28 | Which foods are approved for irradiation in Canada? | Potatoes, onion, wheat and wheat flour, dried spices, and ground beef (approved in 2017). Five items. |
| 29 | How does the list of foods approved for irradiation in Canada compare with that of the US? | Canada's list is short (five items); the US has approved a far longer list of foods for irradiation. |
| 30 | What must appear on the label of an irradiated food? | A statement - 'treated by irradiation', 'treated with radiation', or 'irradiated' - plus the radura symbol, the green stylized plant in a broken circle. |
| 31 | When must an irradiated ingredient be declared as 'irradiated' in the ingredient list of another food? | Only if the irradiated ingredient constitutes 10% or more of the final food. |
| 32 | A frozen turkey dinner contains irradiated dried spices at about 1% of total weight. How must the irradiation be declared? | It does not have to be declared at all, because the irradiated ingredient is less than 10% of the final food. |
| 33 | Is a hazard symbol part of Canadian irradiation labelling? | No. Only the statement (e.g. 'treated by irradiation') and the radura symbol are required; there is no hazard symbol. |
| 34 | Where are Canada's irradiated food processing facilities located? | Port Coquitlam, British Columbia and Laval, Quebec. None are on any UBC campus. |

## Practice MCQs (instructor style)

# FNH 200 — Lesson 10: Ionizing Energy (Food Irradiation) — Practice MCQs

### Q1. What type of energy, shown in the diagram of the electromagnetic spectrum, is used in our everyday cooking?
A. Gamma-ray
B. X-ray
C. Ultraviolet
D. Infrared
E. Microwave

**Answer: D** — Everyday cooking (e.g. a stove element) uses infrared energy; gamma-rays and X-rays are the ionizing region used for food irradiation.

### Q2. Which of the following are approved sources of ionizing energy for food irradiation?
(i) Gamma radiation from Cobalt-60
(ii) Gamma radiation from Cesium-137
(iii) X-rays generated from a machine source at or below 5 MeV
(iv) Electrons generated from a machine source at or below 10 MeV
(v) Microwaves generated from a magnetron

A. (i), (iii)
B. (i), (ii), (v)
C. (iii), (iv)
D. (i), (ii), (iii), (iv)
E. All of the above

**Answer: D** — The three approved sources are gamma from Cobalt-60 or Cesium-137, X-rays at or below 5 MeV, and electrons at or below 10 MeV. Microwaves are non-ionizing and are not an approved irradiation source.

### Q3. The Gray (kiloGray, kGy) used in food irradiation is a measure of:
A. The energy absorbed by a food
B. The energy emitted by the Cobalt-60 source per hour
C. The wavelength of the radiation applied
D. The time the food spends on the conveyor
E. The residual radioactivity remaining in the food

**Answer: A** — Gray (kGy, or Rad) is defined as the energy absorbed by a food, and in Canada the dose must be less than 10 kGy.

### Q4. In a commercial Cobalt-60 irradiation facility, the principal regulatory concern is:
A. Preventing the food from becoming radioactive
B. The safety of the personnel operating the facility
C. Preventing direct contact between the source rods and the food
D. Keeping the water pool free of microbial contamination
E. None of the above

**Answer: B** — The source sits under a water pool and food passes above it on a conveyor; the regulatory concern identified in the lecture is the safety of the personnel, not the food.

### Q5. Direct damage to DNA materials by ionizing energy can lead to which of the following?
A. Destruction of microorganisms and of insects
B. Inactivation of parasites
C. Delaying of ripening and sprouting inhibition
D. Destruction of enzymes
E. All of the above

**Answer: E** — All six listed consequences follow from direct DNA damage: destruction of microorganisms, destruction of insects, inactivation of parasites, delaying of ripening, sprouting inhibition, and destruction of enzymes.

### Q6. Ionizing energy preserves food indirectly by forming which of the following?
(i) Free radicals
(ii) Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2
(iii) Radiative (radioactive) food
(iv) Radiolytic products
(v) Ethylene gas

A. (i), (ii)
B. (i), (ii), (iv)
C. (ii), (iii), (iv)
D. (i), (iv), (v)
E. All of the above

**Answer: B** — The indirect effect splits water to give free radicals, hydrogen peroxide and radiolytic products. Food does not become radioactive, and ethylene is not an irradiation product.

### Q7. A processor irradiates a fresh product at 4 kGy specifically to destroy the spoilage-causing microorganisms and extend shelf life. This treatment is called:
A. Radicidation
B. Radurization
C. Radappertization
D. Radiolysis
E. Radura treatment

**Answer: B** — Radurization (< 10 kGy) targets spoilage-causing microorganisms. Radicidation (< 10 kGy) targets disease-causing microorganisms and radappertization (> 10 kGy) achieves sterilization.

### Q8. Which irradiation treatment could NOT be approved in Canada, and why?
A. Radicidation, because it exceeds 10 kGy
B. Radurization, because it exceeds 10 kGy
C. Radappertization, because it requires more than 10 kGy
D. Radappertization, because it destroys enzymes
E. None of the above; all three are permitted in Canada

**Answer: C** — Radappertization (sterilization) requires greater than 10 kGy, and Canada's permitted dose is less than 10 kGy. Radicidation and radurization are both under 10 kGy.

### Q9. A food manufacturer would like to use ionizing energy for FoodABC. The D value of the targeted microorganism is 0.9 kGy. Assuming it meets all other safety criteria, will the process be approved?
A. Yes
B. No
C. It depends on the pH of the product
D. It depends on whether a Cobalt-60 or a Cesium-137 source is used
E. It depends on whether the food is irradiated frozen or at room temperature

**Answer: C** — An acid food needs 5D = 4.5 kGy (under the limit), but a low-acid food needs 12D = 10.8 kGy, which exceeds Canada's limit of less than 10 kGy. The answer therefore hinges on pH.

### Q10. FoodXYZ is a low-acid food and the D value of its target microorganism is 0.9 kGy. What absorbed dose is required, and is it permitted in Canada?
A. 4.5 kGy; permitted
B. 4.5 kGy; not permitted
C. 9.0 kGy; permitted
D. 10.8 kGy; not permitted
E. 10.8 kGy; permitted

**Answer: D** — Low-acid foods require a 12D process: 12 x 0.9 = 10.8 kGy, which is above Canada's maximum of less than 10 kGy.

### Q11. Which body regulates the USES of radiation sources in Canada?
A. Atomic Energy of Canada Limited
B. Canadian Food Inspection Agency
C. Canadian Irradiation Centre
D. Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission
E. Health Canada, Division 16

**Answer: D** — The Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission regulates the uses of the sources; AECL develops the irradiators and the CFIA handles labelling and advertising of irradiated foods.

### Q12. Which of the following are approved for irradiation in Canada?
(i) Potatoes
(ii) Onion
(iii) Fresh poultry
(iv) Dried spices
(v) Ground beef

A. (i), (ii)
B. (i), (ii), (iii)
C. (i), (ii), (iv), (v)
D. (iii), (iv), (v)
E. All of the above

**Answer: C** — Canada's approved list is potatoes, onion, wheat and wheat flour, dried spices, and ground beef (approved in 2017). Fresh poultry is not on the Canadian list, although the US list is far longer.

### Q13. A frozen turkey dinner contains irradiated dried spices (about 1% of total weight). How should the irradiation treatment be declared?
(i) A statement indicating the food has been treated by irradiation must be shown on the label
(ii) The radura symbol must be shown
(iii) The hazard symbol must be shown

A. (i) only
B. (i) and (ii)
C. (i) and (iii)
D. None of the above, because irradiated food is not regulated in Canada
E. None of the above, because it contains less than 10% irradiated ingredients

**Answer: E** — An irradiated ingredient must be declared only if it makes up 10% or more of the final food; at 1% no declaration is needed. Irradiated food is regulated in Canada, and there is no hazard symbol in irradiation labelling.

### Q14. Where are the irradiated food processing facilities in Canada located?
(i) UBC - Point Grey Campus
(ii) UBC - Great Northern Way Campus
(iii) UBC - Okanagan Campus
(iv) Port Coquitlam, BC
(v) Laval, Quebec

A. (i)
B. (i), (ii)
C. (i), (iv)
D. (iv), (v)
E. (i), (iv), (v)

**Answer: D** — The facilities are in Port Coquitlam, BC and Laval, Quebec. No irradiated food processing facility is located on a UBC campus.

### Q15. Which of the following are precautionary steps used to minimize undesirable changes during food irradiation?
A. Irradiating foods in the frozen state
B. Irradiating under vacuum
C. Adding free radical scavengers such as vitamins C and E
D. A and B only
E. All of the above

**Answer: E** — All three are listed precautionary steps: freezing reduces free water and therefore free radical formation, vacuum removes oxygen, and scavengers (antioxidants) mop up radicals that do form.
