# Lesson 04: Food Standards, Regulations, and Guides — Study Deck

35 flashcards · 12 cloze · 15 MCQs

## Flashcards

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What is the Food and Drugs Act, in full? | R.S.C., 1985, c. F-27 — "An Act respecting food, drugs, cosmetics and therapeutic devices." It is the foundational federal food law in Canada. |
| 2 | Food and Drugs Act Section 3 — what does it prohibit, and for which diseases? | Advertising any food, drug, cosmetic or device to the general public as a treatment, preventative or cure for a Schedule A disease; 3(2) also bans selling a product represented that way. Schedule A includes alcoholism, appendicitis, arthritis, cancer, depression, diabetes, heart disease, hypertension, liver diseases, obesity, sexual impotence, tumours, venereal disease. |
| 3 | Food and Drugs Act Section 4 — what does it prohibit? | No person shall SELL an article of food that: has a poisonous or harmful substance; is unfit for human consumption; consists of any filthy, putrid, disgusting, rotten, decomposed or diseased animal or vegetable substance; is adulterated; or was manufactured, prepared, preserved, packaged or stored under unsanitary conditions. |
| 4 | Food and Drugs Act Section 5 — what does it prohibit? | 5(1): no labelling, packaging, treating, processing, selling or advertising of food that is false, misleading or deceptive, or likely to create an erroneous impression regarding character, value, quantity, composition, merit or safety. 5(2): food not labelled or packaged as the regulations require is deemed contrary to 5(1). |
| 5 | Food and Drugs Act Section 7 — what does it prohibit? | No person shall manufacture, prepare, preserve, package or store for sale any food under unsanitary conditions. |
| 6 | What is the key difference between Section 4 and Section 7 of the Food and Drugs Act? | Section 4 is about SELLING food that is itself harmful, unfit, putrid or adulterated. Section 7 is about the unsanitary CONDITIONS under which food is made, packaged or stored for sale. |
| 7 | Which agency administers the Food and Drugs Act, and what are its regulatory functions? | Health Canada, Health Products and Food Branch (HPFB). It sets standards of identity and composition for foods, administers the Food and Drugs Act, and writes the food additive regulations. |
| 8 | Which agency enforces the Food and Drugs Act, and what are its regulatory functions? | The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA). It provides food inspection services, enforces Acts including the Food and Drugs Act and the Agriculture and Agri-Food Administrative Monetary Penalties Act, and handles food labelling for industry — so accurate labelling is CFIA's responsibility. |
| 9 | Which agency approves food additives such as aspartame and nitrites? | Health Canada — additive approval is administration and rule-setting, not enforcement, so it is not CFIA. |
| 10 | What are the six pieces of information mandatory on every food label in Canada? | 1) Bilingual (English and French); 2) common name; 3) net quantity; 4) name of the business; 5) ingredient list; 6) Nutrition Facts table — all legible, with placement rules. |
| 11 | What label information is required only DEPENDING ON THE FOOD (conditional, not universal)? | Best before date, country of origin, sweeteners such as aspartame, irradiated foods, % milk fat and % alcohol, and allergens. |
| 12 | Of net quantity, % milk fat, % alcohol, Nutrition Facts and ingredient list, which is NOT mandatory on a yogurt label? | % alcohol — it is conditional and applies to alcoholic beverages. % milk fat IS mandatory on yogurt because yogurt is a dairy product. |
| 13 | How many disease risk reduction claims are approved in Canada, and how do they square with Section 3? | Five. Section 3 bans treatment, preventative or CURE claims for Schedule A diseases, but this short approved list of RISK REDUCTION claims is permitted. |
| 14 | Name the five approved disease risk reduction claims in Canada. | 1) Sodium and potassium; 2) calcium and vitamin D; 3) saturated and trans fats; 4) cancer risk reduction; 5) dental caries. |
| 15 | What is the front-of-package (FOP) label in Canada? | A black-and-white magnifying-glass symbol from Health Canada reading "High in / Élevé en," flagging foods high in saturated fat, sugars, and/or sodium on the front of the package. |
| 16 | What is a standardized food, and what are some examples? | A food for which the Food and Drug Regulations prescribe a standard of identity — WHAT must be in it and HOW MUCH. Standardized: bread, enriched white bread, raisin bread, whole wheat bread, ice cream. Not standardized: pizza dough. |
| 17 | Why can a fat-free frozen product not be called "ice cream" in Canada? | Ice cream is a standardized food with a required minimum milk fat content. A product lacking that milk fat must be renamed — hence "frozen dessert." |
| 18 | Name the grades of eggs in Canada. | Canada Grade A, Grade B, Grade C, and Nest Run. Canada Fancy and Canada Choice are NOT egg grades. |
| 19 | Name the grades for processed fruit such as canned peaches, and does brand predict grade? | Canada Fancy, Canada Choice, Canada Standard. Grade is independent of brand — in the class demo a no-name peach product was Canada Fancy while a competing can was Canada Choice. |
| 20 | Name the grades of beef in Canada and what distinguishes them. | Canada A, AA, AAA, and Prime — increasing marbling (fat within the muscle). Canada A requires only that some marbling be visible; Prime is the most marbled. |
| 21 | Roughly how many food additives are approved in Canada versus the United States, and why the difference? | Roughly 300 in Canada versus over 2,600 in the US. The gap is due mainly to differences in the DEFINITION of a food additive, not to different chemicals in the food supply. |
| 22 | What five categories does Canada's definition of "food additive" exclude? | Nutritive materials, seasonings, agricultural residues, food packaging materials, and drugs for therapeutic uses in animals (veterinary drugs). |
| 23 | What is Division 16 of the Food and Drug Regulations? | The tables of permitted food additives, listing for each additive the foods it is permitted in or upon and the maximum level of use and other conditions. Approval is therefore use-specific and level-specific, never general. |
| 24 | What must be submitted to Health Canada to get a new food additive approved? | Composition, properties and method of manufacture; amount and purpose of use; method of analysis; data on intended physical or other technical effect; detailed reporting of tests; a proposed maximum residue limit in the finished food; specimens of the labelling; and a sample of the additive. |
| 25 | Describe the dose-response curve, and how do common foods differ from poisons on it? | Regions: a flat "no effect" zone, a threshold, a dose range of increasing response, then a plateau at maximum effect. Everything has a curve — even common foods — but poisons have a far lower threshold. |
| 26 | What is the Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI)? | The amount of an additive that can safely be consumed daily over a lifetime, derived from the no-effect level for humans on the dose-response curve with safety margins applied. |
| 27 | What is the Probable Daily Intake (PDI)? | The amount of an additive people are actually likely to consume, estimated from real dietary patterns. It must sit well below the ADI. |
| 28 | What happens when the probable daily intake of an additive exceeds the acceptable daily intake? | The additive will not be approved, or will be approved only with restricted use. |
| 29 | Aspartame: who is it for, who must avoid it, and what does it break down into on digestion? | Intended for people with diabetic concerns; people with phenylketonuria (PKU) must avoid it, hence the phenylalanine warning. Digestion yields aspartic acid, methanol, and phenylalanine. |
| 30 | How much aspartame is in a can of diet pop, how many cans reach the ADI, and where does that intake sit on the dose-response curve? | About 200 mg per can, and about 12 cans per day would be needed to reach the ADI. Actual probable daily intake sits far to the left, deep in the flat no-effect region. |
| 31 | Why are nitrites used in cured meats? | All of the above: to develop the pink colour of the finished product, to enhance flavour (meat + nitrite + salt + heat gives cured meat flavour), and to act as an antimicrobial agent. |
| 32 | What is the colour chemistry of cured versus ordinary cooked meat? | Myoglobin (red) + heat gives metmyoglobin (brown). Myoglobin (red) + nitrite (NO2-) gives nitrosylhemochrome, the stable pink of cured ham. |
| 33 | How does nitrite act as an antimicrobial in cured meats? | Nitrite prevents spores of Clostridium botulinum from germinating into growing, toxin-producing cells. |
| 34 | Which chemical found in cured meat is potentially carcinogenic, and how is it formed? | Nitrosamines — formed by nitrite + amines with heat. Not nitrates, nitrites, nitric oxide, or nitrosylhemochrome. |
| 35 | Despite the risk, why is nitrite still approved for cured meat in Canada? | All of the above: foods are not a major source of nitrosamine exposure; the risk appears to be very low; and no suitable replacement has been identified to control Clostridium botulinum. |

- {{c1::Health Canada (Health Products and Food Branch, HPFB)}} ADMINISTERS the Food and Drugs Act, sets standards of identity and composition, and writes the food additive regulations, while {{c2::CFIA (Canadian Food Inspection Agency)}} handles inspection, ENFORCEMENT, and food labelling for industry.
- The six mandatory elements on every Canadian food label are: {{c1::bilingual (English and French)}}, {{c1::common name}}, {{c1::net quantity}}, {{c2::name of the business}}, {{c2::list of ingredients}}, and {{c2::Nutrition Facts table}}; required only depending on the food are {{c3::best before date, country of origin, sweeteners (aspartame), irradiated foods, % milk fat, % alcohol, and allergens}}.

## Practice MCQs (instructor style)

# FNH 200 — Lesson 04 Practice MCQs
## Food Standards, Regulations, and Guides (Food and Drugs Act)

All content drawn from the Lesson 04 lecture deck and podcast episode.

---

### Q1. __(i)__ is responsible for the administration of our Food and Drugs Act, while __(ii)__ is responsible for its enforcement?

A. (i) Health Canada: HPFB; (ii) CFIA
B. (i) Health Canada: HPFB; (ii) Measurement Canada
C. (i) CFIA; (ii) Health Canada: HPFB
D. (i) CFIA; (ii) Industry Canada
E. (i) Measurement Canada; (ii) CFIA

**Answer: A** — Health Canada's Health Products and Food Branch administers the Act, sets standards of identity and composition, and writes the food additive regulations; CFIA provides inspection and enforcement. Option C is the classic reversal trap.

---

### Q2. Which agency below is responsible for accurate labelling of foods?

A. Health Canada: HPFB
B. CFIA
C. Measurement (Industry) Canada
D. BC Ministry of Health
E. City of Vancouver

**Answer: B** — Food labelling for industry sits with CFIA as part of its enforcement role. Health Canada writes the rules but does not police label accuracy.

---

### Q3. Which of the following is NOT mandatory on the label of yogurt?

A. Net quantity
B. % milk fat
C. % alcohol
D. Nutrition Facts table
E. List of ingredients

**Answer: C** — Net quantity, Nutrition Facts and the ingredient list are among the six universally mandatory elements, and % milk fat is required because yogurt is a dairy product. % alcohol is conditional and applies to alcoholic beverages.

---

### Q4. Consider the following pieces of information: (i) common name, (ii) best before date, (iii) net quantity, (iv) country of origin, (v) name of the business. Which are among the six elements mandatory on EVERY prepackaged food label in Canada?

A. (i), (iii), (v)
B. (i), (ii), (iii)
C. (ii), (iv)
D. (i), (ii), (iii), (iv), (v)
E. (iii), (iv), (v)

**Answer: A** — The six universal elements are bilingual labelling, common name, net quantity, name of the business, ingredient list, and Nutrition Facts table. Best before date and country of origin are second-tier items required only depending on the food.

---

### Q5. A cereal box carries the statement "eating this cereal will cure your diabetes." Which section of the Food and Drugs Act does this most directly violate?

A. Section 3
B. Section 4
C. Section 5
D. Section 7
E. None of the above; the claim is permitted because diabetes is on the approved disease risk reduction list

**Answer: A** — Section 3 forbids advertising any food to the general public as a treatment, preventative or cure for a Schedule A disease, and diabetes is on Schedule A. E is wrong twice over: the approved list covers risk *reduction*, not cures, and diabetes is not one of the five approved claims.

---

### Q6. A processor stores raw ingredients in a rodent-infested warehouse but has not yet sold any product. Which section of the Food and Drugs Act applies?

A. Section 3
B. Section 4
C. Section 5
D. Section 7
E. All of the above

**Answer: D** — Section 7 prohibits manufacturing, preparing, preserving, packaging or storing food for sale under unsanitary conditions. Section 4 is the confusable: it targets the act of *selling* food that is harmful, unfit or adulterated, which has not happened here.

---

### Q7. How many disease risk reduction claims are currently approved in Canada according to the CFIA website?

A. Three
B. Four
C. Five
D. Nine
E. There is no approved list; Section 3 prohibits all health-related claims

**Answer: C** — The five approved claims are sodium and potassium, calcium and vitamin D, saturated and trans fats, cancer risk reduction, and dental caries. E confuses the Section 3 ban on cure claims with the separate, permitted category of risk reduction claims.

---

### Q8. Which of the following correctly pairs a food category with its grade names?

A. Eggs: Canada Fancy, Canada Choice, Canada Standard
B. Processed fruit: Canada A, AA, AAA, Prime
C. Beef: Canada Grade A, B, C, and Nest Run
D. Processed fruit: Canada Fancy, Canada Choice, Canada Standard
E. Eggs: Canada A, AA, AAA, Prime

**Answer: D** — Processed fruit such as canned peaches uses Canada Fancy, Choice and Standard. Eggs use Canada Grade A, B, C and Nest Run; beef uses Canada A, AA, AAA and Prime, climbing with marbling, where Canada A needs only some visible marbling. Every other option swaps two of the three systems.

---

### Q9. Due to differences in definitions, there are roughly __(i)__ approved additives in Canada, while over __(ii)__ in the States.

A. (i) 15; (ii) 300
B. (i) 15; (ii) 2600
C. (i) 300; (ii) 2600
D. (i) 2600; (ii) 300
E. (i) 300; (ii) 300, the lists are equivalent

**Answer: C** — Canada permits roughly 300 additives versus over 2,600 in the US, and the difference is definitional rather than a real gap in chemical use. Canada's definition excludes several categories the US counts as additives.

---

### Q10. Canada's definition of "food additive" excludes which of the following? (i) nutritive materials, (ii) seasonings, (iii) agricultural residues, (iv) food packaging materials, (v) drugs for therapeutic uses in animals

A. (i), (ii) only
B. (i), (iii), (v)
C. (ii), (iv), (v)
D. (i), (ii), (iii), (iv), (v)
E. None of the above; Canada's definition excludes nothing

**Answer: D** — All five categories are excluded from the Canadian definition, which is why Canada's count is roughly 300 while the US counts over 2,600. This is a case where "all of the above" is genuinely correct.

---

### Q11. When the probable daily intake of an additive in question is greater than the acceptable daily intake, the additive will:

A. Be approved
B. Not be approved
C. Be approved with restricted use
D. B or C
E. Be approved provided the manufacturer submits a sample and labelling specimens

**Answer: D** — The rule is that PDI must fall well below ADI; if it does not, the additive is either rejected or permitted only for restricted uses. E describes items in the approval dossier, which do not override the safety arithmetic.

---

### Q12. Which of the following about the dose-response curve and additive safety is TRUE?

A. The Acceptable Daily Intake is derived from the maximum-effect plateau of the curve
B. The Probable Daily Intake for aspartame from diet pop lies above the threshold
C. The Acceptable Daily Intake is derived from the no-effect level, with safety margins applied
D. Common foods have no dose-response curve because they are not toxic at any dose
E. All of the above

**Answer: C** — ADI comes from the no-effect level for humans, not the maximum effect. A can of diet pop holds about 200 mg of aspartame and it would take roughly 12 cans a day to reach the ADI, so real intake sits deep in the flat no-effect region; and every substance, including common foods, has a dose-response curve, just with a much higher threshold than a poison.

---

### Q13. Which one of the following chemicals found in cured meat is potentially carcinogenic?

A. Nitrates
B. Nitric oxide
C. Nitrites
D. Nitrosylhemochrome
E. Nitrosamines

**Answer: E** — Nitrosamines are formed when nitrite reacts with amines under heat, and they are the potentially carcinogenic product. Nitrite is the added additive, and nitrosylhemochrome is the harmless pink pigment of cured meat.

---

### Q14. Why do we use nitrites in cured meats?

A. To enhance the pink colour in finished products
B. To enhance flavours
C. To act as an antimicrobial agent
D. All of the above
E. None of the above, as nitrites are not approved to be added to cured meats in Canada

**Answer: D** — Nitrite converts myoglobin to pink nitrosylhemochrome, combines with salt and heat to give cured meat flavour, and blocks Clostridium botulinum spores from germinating into toxin-producing cells. Nitrite is approved in Canada, so E is false.

---

### Q15. Despite the fact that risk is associated with using nitrite in cured meat, why is it still approved in our food system? (i) foods are not a major source of nitrosamine exposure, (ii) the risk appears to be very low, (iii) a suitable replacement of nitrite has not been identified to control C. botulinum, (iv) nitrosamines have been shown to be entirely non-toxic, (v) nitrite is only permitted in unstandardized foods

A. (i), (ii) only
B. (i), (ii), (iii)
C. (iii), (iv), (v)
D. (i), (ii), (iii), (iv), (v)
E. None of the above, as the question is inappropriate; nitrite is not approved in the Canadian food system

**Answer: B** — The three stated justifications are (i), (ii) and (iii). Statement (iv) contradicts the fact that nitrosamines are potentially carcinogenic, and (v) is invented; nitrite is approved, so E is false.
