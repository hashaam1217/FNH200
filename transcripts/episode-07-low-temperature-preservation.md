# Episode 7: Low Temperature Preservation — Ice Is Not Your Friend

*FNH 200 — The Podcast. Episode 7: freezing, and everything it quietly ruins.*

---

**DEV:** Okay, real talk. I found a tub of ice cream at the back of my freezer covered in crunchy ice crystals. Did someone spill water in there?

**MAYA:** Nobody spilled anything. That ice came from *inside* the ice cream — it left, wandered around, and came back as gravel. Today: why your freezer is a slow-motion demolition crew, and why the single most important rule of frozen food isn't "keep it cold."

**DEV:** …It isn't?

**MAYA:** It's "keep it **constant**." Lesson 7. Let's go.

---

## Cold storage: slowing down, not stopping

**MAYA:** Two regimes here. First, **cold storage / refrigeration: −2 to 16 °C** 🎯. What it does: **slows down** microbial growth. Not stops. And **the optimal temperature varies by food** — some produce is actually *damaged* by fridge temperatures.

Then **freezing** — and this is the concept students get wrong most 🎯. Freezing does **NOT kill microorganisms**. It **freezes water**. Microbes go dormant and wake right back up on thawing.

**DEV:** So the exam question "freezing preserves foods by…" — I'd want to pick killing microbes.

**MAYA:** And you'd lose the mark. The correct combination is: **slowing microbial growth, stopping microbial growth, and freezing water molecules** — *not* killing anything, and definitely not "increasing water activity."

**DEV:** Wait — freezing *decreases* water activity, right? Liquid water becomes ice, so it's not available.

**MAYA:** Exactly the link back to Lesson 2. Ice is bound-up water; free water drops; a_w falls. Microbes are surrounded by water they cannot drink. That's the real mechanism.

## Packaging for the cold chain

**MAYA:** Refrigeration teams up with packaging 🎯. Know these:

- **Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP)** — the gas mix inside the package is altered at packing time, then left alone. Those trays of salami, pre-cut fruit, and fresh pasta.
- **Controlled atmosphere packaging** — conditions are actively *maintained*, like the apple storage rooms from Episode 1.
- **Vacuum packaging** — suck the air out. And the exam question 🎯: vacuum packaging is especially effective against **aerobic** spoilage bacteria. Makes sense — remove oxygen, starve the oxygen-dependent spoilers. What it does *not* protect against is anaerobes, which is why vacuum-packed deli meat still needs nitrite and refrigeration.
- **Moisture barrier packaging** — keeps water in or out.

**DEV:** So vacuum packing solves one problem and quietly creates a nicer home for the scariest bug.

**MAYA:** The recurring theme of this course. And even in the fridge, food degrades chemically 🎯 — **syneresis** shows up again. Which refrigerated changes come from syneresis? **Staling of bread** and **drip loss in fish** — water being squeezed out of a gel network. Not the colour change in meat (that's pigment chemistry), not nutrient loss.

## Ice: the villain with a volume problem

**MAYA:** Now freezing proper. Physics fact 🎯: during freezing, the volume of water **increases**. Water is weird — it expands about 9% as it becomes ice.

**DEV:** And if that's happening *inside* a strawberry cell…

**MAYA:** …the cell wall gets shoved, stretched, and sometimes punctured. Ice crystals are little knives. Which sets up the central principle of the whole lesson: **crystal size determines damage.**

- **Rapid / deep freezing** → **many small ice crystals**, formed both inside and outside the cells → minimal structural damage.
- **Slow / mild-temperature freezing** → **few large ice crystals**, mostly *outside* the cells, which grow big and rupture cell walls → mush on thawing.

**DEV:** So the carrot diagram — lots of tiny crystals versus a handful of monsters.

**MAYA:** That's it. Freeze fast, keep the structure. Freeze slow, and when you thaw it you get **drip loss** — all the cell fluid pouring out because the cells are shredded. Exam radar 🎯: drip loss in thawed strawberries results from **large ice crystals formed by fluctuating storage temperature**. Read that carefully — the classic wrong answer is "large crystals during *rapid* freezing," which is self-contradictory.

## The most important factor (it's not what you think)

**MAYA:** Here's the headline question 🎯: *the most important factor in producing high-quality frozen foods.* Options include small crystals, low temperature, and rapid freezing — all tempting. The answer: **keeping the freezing/storage temperature CONSTANT.**

**DEV:** Why does constant beat cold?

**MAYA:** Because of your ice cream. Every time the temperature drifts up, some ice **melts**; when it drops again, that water **refreezes** — but not where it came from. It migrates and joins existing crystals, so crystals get bigger and bigger with every cycle. This is **recrystallization**, driven by **migration of water molecules** 🎯. That's literally the exam answer for ice crystals on badly stored ice cream: migration of water molecules, caused by temperature fluctuation. A rock-steady −18 °C beats a fluctuating −25 °C every time.

**DEV:** So opening the freezer door forty times to stare at nothing…

**MAYA:** …is you personally manufacturing ice crystals. And the other packaging-related casualty 🎯: **freeze burn** — those dried, discoloured, leathery patches on frozen meat and chicken. It's **sublimation**: ice going straight to vapour and escaping through inadequate packaging, leaving dehydrated tissue behind. Not "freeze cooking," not "freeze dehydration" — **freeze burn**.

## Freezing methods: pick your weapon

**MAYA:** Four methods 🎯:

1. **Air-blast freezing** — cold air blasted over the food. Includes **fluidized-bed freezers** producing **IQF, individually quick frozen** product. The known question: **strawberries, mango chunks, and shrimp** are best frozen by **IQF in fluidized-bed freezers**. That's why frozen berries pour out loose instead of arriving as one strawberry brick.
2. **Indirect contact freezing** — food touches a cold surface but never the refrigerant. **Plate freezers** and **slush freezers** (yes, the slushie machine is food-science equipment).
3. **Immersion freezing** — the food, usually already packaged, is dunked into a cold liquid. Very fast, great contact. The known question: a **turkey** — big, bagged, irregular — is frozen by **immersion freezing**.
4. **Cryogenic freezing** — the extreme end: liquid nitrogen or CO₂ for near-instant freezing.

**DEV:** Berries get the fluidized bed, turkeys get dunked.

**MAYA:** And packaging for frozen goods 🎯 — which material is NOT suitable? **Glass bottles.** Water expands on freezing, glass doesn't, glass shatters. Cardboard and plastic film are standard; foil film and Tetra Pak are less common but workable. Glass is the outright wrong answer.

## Thawing: the neglected half

**MAYA:** Last piece. Thawing rate matters as much as freezing rate, and thawing is *slower* than freezing — water conducts heat better than ice, so the thawed outer layer insulates the frozen core. That warm outer layer sitting at room temperature is prime microbial real estate, and remember: those microbes were never killed, just paused. This is exactly why you thaw in the fridge, not on the counter.

**DEV:** And the shelf-life ranking from the lecture — the three milks?

**MAYA:** Perfect summary of the whole preservation arc so far. Raw-ish **pasteurized milk + refrigeration** = shortest, days. Pasteurized carton, same deal. **UHT-sterilized Parmalat** = months on a shelf, no fridge. More heat up front buys more time later.
