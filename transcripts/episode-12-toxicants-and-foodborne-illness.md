## Toxicity, hazard, risk

**MAYA:** Three definitions that sound like synonyms and aren't 🎯:

- **Toxicity** — how "bad" or toxic a substance intrinsically is.
- **Hazard** — the probability of **contacting** something toxic.
- **Risk** — how likely a hazard actually **happens**, plus the **impact** if it does.

**DEV:** So something can be wildly toxic and still low risk if you never encounter it.

**MAYA:** Botulinum toxin is among the most toxic substances known — **LD₅₀ around 1 nanogram per kilogram of body weight** — and yet almost nobody gets botulism, because the hazard of contact is tiny. High toxicity, low risk.

And the **dose-response curve** returns from Lesson 4 🎯 — no effect, then a threshold, then rising response, then maximum effect. The dose makes the poison.

**MAYA:** Now that ranking. The lecture's table compares **popular notion** against **regulatory concern**, and they run in **opposite directions** 🎯:

| Food component | Popular notion | Regulatory concern |
|---|---|---|
| Food additives | **Great** | **Minimal** |
| Agricultural chemicals | ↑ | ↓ |
| Natural toxicants | | |
| Environmental contaminants | | |
| Nutritional properties | | |
| Microbiological contaminants | **Minimal** | **Great** |

**DEV:** So the public panics about additives and shrugs at bacteria, and the regulators do the reverse.

**MAYA:** That inversion is one of the most quotable facts in FNH 200. Bacteria kill people every year in Canada. Approved additives, at approved levels, don't.

## The toxicant table: three columns

**MAYA:** Now the big classification table 🎯 — three categories, and the exam asks you to sort examples into them.

**Column 1 — Naturally occurring toxicants: CONSTITUENTS.** These are *part of the food itself*:
- **Cholinesterase inhibitors** — **solanine in potatoes**
- **Cyanogenic glycosides** — **amygdalin in apple seeds** (and bitter almonds, apricot kernels)
- **Glucosinolates** — **sinigrin in cabbage**
- **Protease inhibitors** — in **soybeans**
- **Nitrites** — in **green leafy plants**
- **Allergens** — **beta-lactoglobulin in milk, peanut proteins, soy proteins**

**Column 2 — Naturally occurring toxicants: CONTAMINANTS.** Produced by *something living on or in* the food:
- **Mycotoxins** — from moulds: **aflatoxin, patulin, ochratoxin A, vomitoxin, zearalenone**. Examples: **aflatoxin in mouldy peanuts, vomitoxin in wheat.**
- **Bacterial toxins** — **botulinum toxin, staphylococcal toxin**
- **Seafood toxins** — **histamine, saxitoxin, domoic acid, tetrodotoxin**

**Column 3 — Environmental toxicants.** Introduced from outside:
- **Food packaging residues** — monomers of plastic resins
- **Pesticide residues** — herbicides, insecticides, fungicides; e.g. malathion on fruit
- **Heavy metals** — **lead, mercury, cadmium**; e.g. lead in vegetables, **mercury in large ocean fish like swordfish**
- **Animal drugs** — antibiotics, hormones; e.g. **penicillin in milk**
- **Radioisotopes** — from soil or radioactive fallout

**DEV:** So the two exam questions I can already see coming: **allergens like nuts** are…

**MAYA:** **Natural constituents** — the protein is part of the nut. And **toxins on mouldy nuts** are **natural contaminants** — aflatoxin, made by a mould that moved in. Same nut, two different columns depending on whether the toxin is the food or a squatter 🎯.

**DEV:** And the Fukushima question?

**MAYA:** The 2011 Fukushima Daiichi meltdown introduced **environmental** toxicants — radioisotopes — into the food chain. Not constituent, not contaminant. Environmental sources sort into **industrial activity** (mercury, lead, dioxins, PCBs, radioisotopes), **agricultural activity** (pesticide, herbicide, antibiotic residues), **food processing** (packaging residues, cleaning supplies, equipment maintenance), and **naturally occurring** environmental toxicants (mercury, radioisotopes).

## Four toxicants worth knowing by name

**MAYA:** Specific ones the lecture drills 🎯:

**Solanine — green potatoes.** Normal level: **2–13 mg per 100 g** of potato. In the **green part: 80–100 mg**. It's a **cholinesterase inhibitor**, it's **heat resistant** — cooking will not save you — and symptoms run drowsiness, vomiting, diarrhea, sensitivity, difficulty breathing, and in extreme cases **death**. Minimize risk through **farming and storage practices** (keep potatoes dark, don't let them green).

**Amygdalin — the almond question.** The toxin naturally found in (bitter) almonds is **amygdalin, a cyanogenic glycoside**. Health Canada has issued warnings about **apricot kernels** for exactly this — cyanide poisoning.

**Domoic acid — the Canadian one.** 🎯 "Name the neurotoxin first discovered on **Prince Edward Island**, where severely affected people suffered **memory loss**." That's **domoic acid** — amnesic shellfish poisoning. Not saxitoxin (paralytic shellfish poisoning), not tetrodotoxin (pufferfish), not histamine (scombroid). The PEI + memory loss combination is the giveaway. BC runs a **Shellfish Harvesting Status Map** through the BCCDC precisely to track these closures.

**Mould on food.** 🎯 "We can trim off the tiny mould grown on the food" — true, but it **depends on storage temperature, the texture of the food, AND the size of the mould**. All three. Hard cheese, small spot: trim generously and it's fine. Soft, moist food: the mould's invisible threads — and its toxins — have already spread throughout. Throw it out.

## Outbreak, intoxication, infection

**MAYA:** Now to illness. Foodborne illness comes from **microorganisms, parasites, plants, animals, and chemicals**.

Definition question 🎯: *"a situation in which two or more people experience a similar illness after ingestion of the same food, and epidemiological evidence implicates the food as the source"* — that's an **outbreak**. Not an incident, not a single case. **Two or more people** is the threshold.

The Canadian case study: the **Maple Leaf Foods 2008 outbreak** — **listeriosis in cold cuts**, **57 confirmed cases and 22 deaths**.

**DEV:** Twenty-two people died from deli meat.

**MAYA:** Which is why we tolerate nitrite. Now the crucial split:

**Food INTOXICATION** — the **toxin is already in the food** before you eat it. The organism did its work in the food; you're poisoned by the chemical, not the germ.
**Food INFECTION** — the **living organism** is what you swallow, and it multiplies inside you.

That distinction predicts the symptoms and the timing.

## The organism table

**MAYA:** Learn these five plus two viruses 🎯 — organism, onset, foods, prevention.

**INTOXICATIONS**

***Clostridium botulinum*.** Botulinal toxins are **among the most toxic substances known** — LD₅₀ ≈ **1 ng/kg**. Symptoms: **dizziness, double vision, difficulty swallowing, respiratory or cardiac paralysis, death — 12 to 36 hours.** Foods: **low-acid foods in anaerobic conditions**, improperly home-canned, vacuum-packed, poorly refrigerated foods, and **baked potatoes wrapped in foil**. Spores turn up in carrots, potatoes (soil contamination), fresh and processed meats, **honey and corn syrup**. Prevention: the **"botulinum cook" (12D)**, **sodium nitrite** in cured meats, refrigerating vegetable and fruit juices, and — critically — **no honey, corn syrup, or molasses for infants under 1 year**, because **pasteurizing honey does NOT inactivate the spores**.

**DEV:** Baked potato in foil. That's a *normal thing people do*.

**MAYA:** Foil wrap creates an anaerobic pocket; leave it at room temperature and you've built a botulism incubator. Refrigerate it or unwrap it.

***Escherichia coli* O157:H7 (Shiga toxin).** Symptoms: **bloody diarrhea, kidney failure, hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS)** in severe cases — **12 to 72 hours**. Foods: **undercooked ground meat, raw milk, unpasteurized apple juice/cider, lettuce, spinach, alfalfa sprouts**, and contaminated water — the **Walkerton, Ontario** outbreak. Prevention: sanitation and handwashing; it's killed by **normal cooking, pasteurization, and ionizing radiation**, but it **survives acidic conditions** (hence the apple juice cases) and **doesn't grow below 8 °C**.

**INFECTIONS**

***Salmonella* spp.** Symptoms: **nausea, fever, vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhea — 6 to 48 hours.** Foods: raw meats and poultry, eggs, milk, dairy. The number that will haunt you 🎯: **about 60% of chickens sold at retail are contaminated with *Salmonella*.** Prevention: **easily killed by heat** (normal cooking, pasteurization) and ionizing energy — **but it survives freezing and dehydration**, so **frozen-and-thawed poultry is just as contaminated as fresh**.

***Listeria monocytogenes*.** Symptoms: **flu-like symptoms, meningitis, septicemia, pneumonia, birth defects, stillbirth — 1 day to 3 WEEKS.** Foods: raw milk, dairy, vegetables, fish, meat. The dangerous trait 🎯: it **grows slowly on foods during refrigerated storage** and is **fairly tolerant to salt**. Killed easily by cooking and pasteurization. Keep raw materials and high-risk foods **below 4 °C**.

**DEV:** A pathogen that grows in the fridge. That's the Maple Leaf one.

**MAYA:** Cold cuts, refrigerated, ready-to-eat, no kill step before serving. Listeria's whole niche.

**VIRUSES**

**Hepatitis A.** Fever, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fatigue, **swelling of the liver, jaundice** — **10 to 50 days**. Foods prepared with human contact; contaminated water. Prevention: handwashing, personal hygiene, avoid raw seafood.

**Norovirus.** Vomiting, diarrhea, headache, fever — **24 to 48 hours**. Sewage, contaminated water, salad ingredients, raw seafood, **infected food workers**. Prevention: potable water, hygiene, cook all seafood.

**DEV:** Both are basically "someone didn't wash their hands."

## Ten rules and the temperature danger zone

**MAYA:** Judy said this explicitly: for a summer class of mostly non-food-scientists, she cares more that you can **apply** the WHO/BC Health Files **ten rules for safe food handling** than that you memorize theory 🎯:

Choose foods processed for safety (e.g. pasteurized over raw) · cook foods thoroughly · eat cooked foods immediately · store food promptly and carefully · **reheat cooked foods thoroughly** · **avoid cross-contamination** between raw food and cooked food · wash hands repeatedly · keep kitchen surfaces clean · don't let anyone with diarrhea or infected sores prepare food · wash all fruits and vegetables · and **if in doubt, throw it out.**

And the number to tattoo next to pH 4.6 🎯: the **Temperature Danger Zone (TDZ) is 4 °C to 60 °C (40–140 °F)**. Bacteria grow and produce toxins in that range. Keep cold food below 4, hot food above 60, and minimize time in between.

## HACCP: how industry does it

**MAYA:** Last piece — the industrial system 🎯. **HACCP: Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point**, and the fun origin fact: **created by NASA** to guarantee astronauts' food was safe. Seven steps:

1. **Identify hazards**
2. **Determine the critical control points (CCPs)**
3. **Establish control measures** — critical limits or thresholds
4. **Establish procedures to monitor CCPs**
5. **Establish corrective actions**
6. **Keep records**
7. **Verify procedures**

Worked example for canned food — and notice how it recaps Lesson 6 perfectly: hazard = ***C. botulinum* spores**; CCP = **canning**; control measures = **temperature and time**; monitoring = **thermometer at the cold spot, data logging**; corrective action = **calculate the additional time needed**; then records and verification.

**DEV:** The cold spot shows up again.

**MAYA:** Everything in this course eventually points back at the coldest point in the can. And on hazard categories 🎯: the recognized types are **biological, chemical, physical, and allergenic** — "environmental" is not one of the standard HACCP hazard categories.
