## Biotechnology: much older than you think

**MAYA:** Definition first. **Biotechnology** is the use of living organisms — or their parts — to make products. Which means it covers ancient fermentation *and* modern genetic engineering.

Exam radar 🎯 — a known question asks which of canola, cheese, insulin, and papaya is NOT a biotechnological product. And the correct answer is **none of them: all are biotech products.** GM canola, GM papaya (engineered for virus resistance), insulin made by engineered bacteria, and cheese — the ancient one — all belong.

**DEV:** So the answer is "all of the above" in disguise.

**MAYA:** Judy loves those. Another 🎯: examples of biotechnology products — **beer, bovine rennin, flavouring ingredients, miso, soy sauce, xanthan gum**? **All of them.** Also on that list: **chymosin**, **vitamins**, **invertase**, **glutamic acid** — MSG's parent — and probiotic drinks like Yakult. The theme: a huge slice of the grocery store is microbially manufactured.

## Fermentation: the table you should memorize

**MAYA:** Table 9.1 🎯 — microorganism → commodity → product. The exam matches these:

**Lactic acid bacteria** — the workhorse. Cucumbers → **dill and sour pickles**. Olives → green and ripe olives. Cabbage → **sauerkraut and kimchi**. Milk → kishk. Coffee cherries → **coffee beans**. Vanilla beans → **vanilla**. Meat → **salami**. Dairy → **sour cream and yogurt**.

**DEV:** Wait — coffee and vanilla are *fermented*? I thought they were just dried.

**MAYA:** Both go through a fermentation step, and it's a favourite exam curveball. Continuing:

**Lactic acid bacteria + propionic acid bacteria** → **Swiss, Emmenthaler, Gruyère**. Those are the **holey** cheeses — propionic acid bacteria produce CO₂ gas, and the bubbles become the eyes.

**Lactic acid bacteria + mould** → **tempeh and soy sauce** from vegetable products; **Roquefort, Camembert, Brie, and blue cheeses** from dairy.

**Acetic acid bacteria** — grapes → **vinegar**. Remember the apple story from Episode 1: yeast makes ethanol, then acetic acid bacteria turn ethanol into acetic acid. Two different bugs, two steps.

**Yeasts** — malt → **beer, ale, stout**; fruit → **wine, vermouth**; wine → **brandy**; rice → **saké**; bread dough → **bread**.

**DEV:** So the alcohol question 🎯 — which microorganism makes alcoholic beverages and how?

**MAYA:** **Yeast; it converts glucose to ethanol.** Not lactic acid bacteria, not "ethanol to acetic acid" — that's the vinegar step, and it's the trap answer.

## How fermentation actually preserves food

**MAYA:** Three principles of preservation by fermentation 🎯:

1. **Microbial antagonism** — and here's the definition question: it **encourages the growth of alcohol- and acid-producing microorganisms while suppressing spoilage-causing microorganisms.** You deliberately stack the deck so the good bugs win the territory war.
2. **Antimicrobial agents** — the good bugs secrete compounds that inhibit competitors.
3. **Lowering pH** — lactic acid drops the pH, which both preserves and (Lesson 6 callback) puts the food below 4.6.

**DEV:** So it's less "adding a preservative" and more "hiring a bouncer."

**MAYA:** A bouncer that also makes the food taste better. Two more concepts: a **starter culture** is the deliberate inoculation of the right organisms — commercial yeast, a yogurt spoonful, a sourdough starter — instead of hoping the right microbes wander in. And you must create a **favourable environment**: right **temperature, salt, oxygen, and moisture**. The kimchi recipe is exactly this — salt the cabbage, pack it tight to exclude oxygen, hold it at the right temperature, and the lactic acid bacteria take over.

## Cheese: the full pathway

**MAYA:** Now the centrepiece. Judy explicitly wants **the sequence of microbiological, chemical, and physical changes converting milk to cheese**. Here it is:

1. **Pasteurize** the milk.
2. Add the **lactic acid culture** (plus any specialty organisms).
3. Add the **coagulating enzyme** — rennet, or a rennin-like substitute.
4. **Fermentation**: lactic acid bacteria convert **lactose → lactic acid**, dropping the pH.
5. **Curd coagulation**: **casein micelles**, normally stable in calcium, become calcium-sensitive and **form a gel**.
6. **Cut and cook** the gel — this drives out liquid.
7. Split into **curd** and **whey**.
   - **Curd**: casein, fat, most of the calcium and phosphorus, lactic acid, water.
   - **Whey**: whey proteins, water-soluble vitamins, some calcium and phosphorus, water, lactose, lactic acid.
8. The curd is **matted, milled, salted, pressed, cured and ripened**, then packaged.

**DEV:** And whey is just… discarded?

**MAYA:** Big industry problem 🎯 — the lecture flags **whey disposal as a major challenge for the dairy industry** because of its high water and solids content. Hence whey protein powder: the same "turn your waste into a value-added product" move as apple pomace → pectin.

**DEV:** Everything in this course is somebody's garbage becoming somebody's product.

## Rennet, chymosin, and the label mystery

**MAYA:** The enzyme questions 🎯 — three of them, and they're all distinct.

**What enzyme, and where is it naturally found?** **Rennet**, found in the **fourth stomach of a calf** (the abomasum). Chymosin is the active enzyme *within* rennet; the pairing the exam wants is **rennet + 4th stomach of a calf**.

**What is rennet's major function?** **Formation of a yoghurt-like coagulum** — it curdles the milk. Careful: converting lactose to lactic acid is what the *starter culture* does, not rennet. Two different jobs, two different agents.

**DEV:** So the bacteria acidify and the enzyme coagulates.

**MAYA:** And that raises the industrial problem: you cannot possibly source enough calf stomachs for the world's cheese. **So how do we get enough rennet?** Genetic engineering. **Chymosin A is produced by *E. coli* K-12; chymosin B by *Aspergillus niger* or *Kluyveromyces marxianus*** — the gene for calf chymosin was inserted into microbes that now brew it in tanks. The Food and Drug Regulations (Division 8, B.08.033) list every permitted cheese enzyme.

**DEV:** And on the label that shows up as…

**MAYA:** 🎯 **"Microbial enzyme."** Check any Swiss, Camembert, or blue cheese package — pasteurized milk, salt, bacterial culture, calcium chloride, **microbial enzyme**. That's engineered chymosin. It also means most supermarket cheese is technically vegetarian.

**MAYA:** One more cheese question 🎯: **what is NOT a major reason to salt cheese?** Salting *does* draw whey out of the curd, flavour the product, and inhibit spoilage organisms. What it does **not** do is **aid the functions of rennet and the starter culture** — by then those have finished their work, and salt actually slows them down.

## The specialty organisms

**MAYA:** Match the microbe to the cheese 🎯:

- ***Propionibacterium shermanii*** → **Swiss/Emmenthaler**. Produces CO₂ → the **holes** (properly, "eyes") and a sweet, nutty flavour.
- ***Penicillium camembertii*** → **Camembert and Brie**. Grows the **white velvet coating** on the rind and **hydrolyzes protein** to create the **creamy** texture.
- ***Penicillium roquefortii*** → **Roquefort and blue cheeses**. Gives the **blue veins** and hydrolyzes **milk fat** for that sharp, piquant flavour.

**DEV:** Camembert hydrolyzes protein, Roquefort hydrolyzes fat. Different molecules, different cheeses.

**MAYA:** That's exactly the discrimination the exam is testing. Check a real blue cheese label and you'll see *P. roquefortii* and lipase listed as ingredients.

## Genetic modification and Canadian regulation

**MAYA:** Last section — the modern end of biotechnology. Two agencies 🎯: **Health Canada and the CFIA** regulate biotechnology-derived products in Canada. Not the WHO, not the FAO, not Codex — the same domestic pair from Lesson 4.

Canada's key legal category is **novel foods** 🎯, and it's an all-of-the-above: **(i) products never before used as a food, (ii) foods from a process not previously used for food, and (iii) foods modified by genetic manipulation.** All three. Note that GM is only *one* of three routes — a brand-new process makes a food "novel" even with zero genetic engineering.

**DEV:** Examples?

**MAYA:** From Health Canada's approved list — insect-resistant corn, algal protein, purple tomato, acrylamide-reducing yeast, non-browning apple, in-shell pasteurized egg, high-pressure-processed egg products. Judy explicitly said you **don't** need to memorize which specific products are approved — know the *category* definition instead.

**DEV:** And Canada's position overall?

**MAYA:** Regulate by **product, not by process** — assess the safety of what actually ends up on the plate rather than banning a technique outright. The lecture closes by asking you to **articulate your own values** about biotechnology in food — which, given that cheese, beer, insulin and soy sauce are all on the list, is a more interesting question than "GMO good or bad."
