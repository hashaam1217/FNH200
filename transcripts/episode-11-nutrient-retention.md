## Processing: the honest ledger

**MAYA:** First, the balanced view — you should be able to **list benefits versus risks of food processing** 🎯.

**Benefits:** food safety, year-round availability regardless of season or climate, longer shelf life, convenience, less waste, and — the underrated one — **better nutrient bioavailability**.

**Risks:** loss of some nutrients (especially heat- and water-sensitive vitamins), and changes to flavour, colour, and texture.

And there's a framing point that surprises people: **raw foods vary enormously in nutrient content anyway** — genetics, climate, soil, ripeness at harvest — sometimes more than processing changes them. Comparing "processed vs fresh" isn't comparing a fixed number to a reduced one.

**DEV:** And there's the waste angle from Episode 1 — the lecture shows XL Foods dumping tonnes of recalled beef, and a garbage bin full of produce.

**MAYA:** Processing exists partly so that food gets eaten instead of binned. A vitamin that reaches your mouth beats a vitamin that rots in a truck.

## Processing can *add* nutrition

**MAYA:** Now the counterintuitive part 🎯 — **processing increases the bioavailability of nutrients by destroying** three antinutritional factors. It's an all-of-the-above:

- **Amylase inhibitors** — plus trypsin inhibitors — found in cereal grains, peas and beans. Heat kills them, improving digestibility of proteins and carbohydrates.
- **Thiaminase** — an enzyme in fish and shellfish that **destroys thiamin**. Cook the fish, save the vitamin B1.
- **Avidin** — in **raw egg white**, it binds **biotin** and some iron and makes them biologically unavailable. Cooking destroys avidin.

**DEV:** So raw egg whites in a smoothie actively steal your biotin.

**MAYA:** And raw fish comes with an enzyme that eats your thiamin. Heat also improves **digestibility of starch (gelatinization) and protein (denaturation)**, frees up **bound niacin** in cereals, and makes food more **palatable** — which means people actually eat it.

## Which nutrients survive?

**MAYA:** Now stability. Exam radar 🎯: **which nutrient is MOST stable under thermal processing** — carbohydrates, protein, fat, vitamins, or minerals? **Minerals.** They're elements; you can't cook an atom to death. They can *leach* into cooking water, but they don't degrade. Vitamins are the fragile ones.

Then Newsome's **Table 2**, which cross-references each vitamin against neutral pH, acid pH, alkaline pH, oxygen, light, and heat. The exam question 🎯: **which vitamin is LEAST stable at normal food pH (below 7)?** Options: A, C, E. The answer is **vitamin A** — it's unstable in acid, while C and E are stable there.

**DEV:** That's counterintuitive — I'd have said vitamin C for anything.

**MAYA:** Vitamin C is the famously fragile one *overall* — unstable at neutral pH, in alkali, in oxygen, in light, and in heat. But in **acid** specifically, it's stable. That's exactly why the question specifies pH under 7 — it's testing whether you read the table or guessed. Broad patterns worth holding:

- **Vitamin C** — unstable almost everywhere except acid. The classic casualty.
- **Vitamin A and beta-carotene** — unstable in acid, oxygen, light, heat.
- **Vitamin E** — unstable in oxygen, light, heat.
- **Thiamin (B1)** — unstable in alkali and heat.
- **Riboflavin** — notably unstable in **light** (why milk isn't sold in clear glass).
- **Niacin (B2 group)** and **pyridoxine (B6)** — comparatively **stable** across the board.
- **Mineral salts** — stable everywhere.

The grouping Judy asks for 🎯: **vitamins A, C, and E are the vulnerable group; thiamin, niacin, and pyridoxine are the more stable B-vitamin group** — with thiamin the weakest of that trio.

## How much gets lost — the three tables

**MAYA:** Tables 3, 4 and 5 give real percentages 🎯:

**Table 3 — blanching.** In **water**: vitamin C 16–58% lost, riboflavin 30–50%, thiamin 16–34%, niacin 32–37%. With **steam**: vitamin C only 16–26%, vitamin B6 21%.

**DEV:** Steam beats water. Because water-soluble vitamins leach out into the water.

**MAYA:** That's the single most useful principle in the lesson. **Table 4 — conventional canning**: vitamin C **33–90%** lost, thiamin 16–83%, riboflavin 25–67%, niacin 0–75%, folacin 35–84%, pantothenic acid 30–85%, B6 0–91%, biotin 0–78%, vitamin A 0–84%.

**Table 5 — HTST/aseptic vs conventional thermal.** Strained lima beans: **15.8% thiamin lost with HTST vs 40.3% conventional.** Strained beef: 9.5% vs 21.6%. Tomato juice concentrate: 0% vs 2.8%.

**DEV:** So high-temperature-short-time beats the long slow cook — Lesson 6's Z-value logic paying off nutritionally.

**MAYA:** Exactly the connection. Hotter and faster kills the microbes just as well and spares the vitamins. So the exam question 🎯 — **which processing causes the MOST vitamin C loss?** **Commercial sterilization** (conventional canning), at up to 90%. Not blanching, not HTST, not UHT.

## And the winner is: frozen

**MAYA:** Now your aunt's argument 🎯. **Which processing leads to the LEAST nutrient loss?** Options: pasteurization, UHT, refrigeration, freezing, irradiation. The answer, in Newsome's own words: **freezing** — it **"results in a nutritive quality closest to that of fresh, raw foods."** That's literally an explicit learning objective: *identify the one preservation method that results in nutritive quality closest to fresh, raw foods.*

**DEV:** Frozen peas beat fresh peas that spent a week in a truck.

**MAYA:** Often, yes — because processed-from-freshly-harvested beats "market fresh" that was mishandled in transit. Newsome says that outright. The caveats for freezing: small vitamin C losses during the **blanching** step beforehand, and a 10–20% unexplained loss of water-soluble vitamins in pork. And the conditions matter — Episode 7's rules return: freeze **quickly and solidly**, hold at a **constant low temperature (−18 °F or lower)**, and avoid intermittent thawing, which causes drip loss and greater nutrient loss. Frozen shelf life is 9 months to a year — shorter than canned — because not all the water freezes and vitamins A, C and E slowly oxidize.

**MAYA:** One vocabulary question from the article 🎯: **what process is also known as "cold sterilization"?** **Irradiation.** It sterilizes without heat — hence "cold." Not freeze drying, not IQF.

## Storage and home cooking

**MAYA:** Two practical lists. **Minimizing loss during storage** 🎯 — the question is phrased as which should NOT be used, and the answer is **storage at HIGH temperature**. The good practices: **low temperature storage, antioxidants, protective coatings, vacuum packaging, and packaging in tin foil** (blocking light and oxygen — same logic as the chip bag). Newsome notes canned goods stored up to a year below 70–75 °F lose less than 10–20% of their vitamins, but storage *above* room temperature markedly degrades them.

**And at home** — you should be able to name at least five 🎯: don't overcook; use **steaming or microwaving instead of boiling**; use **minimal water**; **keep the cooking liquid** for sauces and soups; **leave skins on** where possible; cut into **larger pieces** to reduce surface area; cook **just before serving** rather than reheating repeatedly; and store produce cold and covered.

**DEV:** Everything reduces to "don't leach it into water you then pour down the sink."

## Restoration, fortification, enrichment

**MAYA:** Last vocabulary set, and it's three near-synonyms with distinct legal meanings 🎯:

- **Restoration** — adding back what was **lost during processing**.
- **Fortification** — adding something **not naturally present** in that food.
- **Enrichment** — similar to fortification, but done **to meet a standard**, and usually at levels **higher than naturally present**.

Worked examples from the slides: **orange juice with added calcium and vitamin D** — OJ doesn't naturally contain those, so it's **fortified**. **Apple juice labelled "100% vitamin C"** — vitamin C is naturally in the fruit and lost in processing, so it's **restored**. **Five Roses white flour marked "ENRICHED"** — milling strips the bran and germ, and the regulations require specific nutrients back at set levels, so it's **enriched**.

**DEV:** Restore = put back. Fortify = add new. Enrich = add to a legal standard.

**MAYA:** And a bonus fact you'll like: in Canada, **vitamin D in milk is mandatory** — 300 to 400 IU per reasonable daily intake — and **skim milk must also have vitamin A added**, 1,200 to 2,500 IU, because removing the fat removes the fat-soluble vitamin A. Judy noted the detailed mandatory-vs-voluntary fortification tables are **not on the final exam**, but the milk case is a nice illustration of the principle.

## The lasagna question

**MAYA:** Finally — Judy showed a **sample final exam question**, so this is as close to a leak as you get. A tray of **lasagna**, and you analyze how cooking (and separately, freezing) affects each component:

- **Spinach → vitamin C.** Heating at pH above 4.6: unstable → significant loss, worse if boiled in lots of water.
- **Tomato → vitamin A.** Tomato is acidic, and vitamin A is unstable in acid and heat → losses.
- **Beef → fat and protein.** Fat can render out and oxidize; protein **denatures**, which actually **improves digestibility**.
- **Cheese → calcium.** A mineral → **stable**. No loss.
- **Pasta → carbohydrate and thiamin.** Cooking **gelatinizes** starch and destroys **amylase inhibitors**, improving bioavailability; thiamin is heat-sensitive and leaches into the cooking water.

**DEV:** So the answer isn't "cooking destroys nutrition" — it's nutrient by nutrient, some down, some up.

**MAYA:** That's the whole thesis of Lesson 11, and if a version of this shows up on your exam, structure your answer exactly that way: name the nutrient, state its stability from Table 2, then say what the process does to it.
