## Fat replacers: three ways to fake butter

**MAYA:** First, why bother? Fat is **9 calories per gram** — the most calorie-dense component in food — and it hides everywhere: peanut butter, soup, yogurt, ice cream, granola bars. If you want the creamy mouthfeel without the calories, you need a stand-in. Exam radar 🎯: fat substitutes come in **three types, classified by what they're made of** — and each has a signature digestibility. This is a guaranteed matching question.

**One: protein-based — Simplesse.** Made from soy, milk, or egg-white protein, put through a process called **microparticulation** — grinding protein into tiny *spherical* particles. Those little spheres roll over your tongue and mimic fat's creaminess. Digestibility: **partially digestible** — it's protein, your body handles it.

**DEV:** So it's fake fat made of real food.

**MAYA:** Right — and a labelling wrinkle the exam loves: products don't have to say "Simplesse"; it **may appear in the ingredient list as "modified protein."** One more trap: can you deep-fry with Simplesse?

**DEV:** …Protein plus high heat equals cooked egg. So, no?

**MAYA:** Exactly — heat wrecks it. Remember that for the finale.

**Two: carbohydrate-based — Maltrins,** a.k.a. maltodextrins: short chains of starch. They hold water and give body and thickness. Digestibility: **fully digestible** — it's just starch fragments.

**Three: fat-based — Olean™,** generic name **olestra**. This is the Pringles molecule. Chemically it's a combination of "sugar" and "fat" — a sucrose core with fatty acids bolted all over it. The molecule is so bulky your digestive enzymes can't grab it. Digestibility: **indigestible.** Zero calories, and — because it's structurally still a fat — it's **heat-stable and can deep-fry.**

**DEV:** So the matching answer is: **Simplesse partially digestible, Maltrin fully digestible, Olean indigestible.** And only Olean can fry.

**MAYA:** Gold star. Now the asterisks. 🎯 Olestra passes through undigested — and as it goes, it **dissolves the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K and carries them out with it**. It inhibits their absorption. That's why olestra products carried the statement "*Vitamins A, D, E and K have been added*" — compensating for what the molecule steals.

**DEV:** And the other, um… digestive side effects I've heard legends about?

**MAYA:** Widely reported, let's say. Which brings us to the exam radar 🎯 Canadian angle: which olestra products are commercially available in Canada? **None.** Not chips, not anything — olestra is not approved here. Fat-free Pringles were an American phenomenon. If a question offers "all of the above," resist — the answer is *none* of the above.

## Sugar substitutes: the sweetness impersonators

**MAYA:** Sugar's turn. First, a regulatory distinction that previews Lesson 4 🎯: under Canada's Food and Drug Regulations, **sweetening AGENTS** — Division 18 — are the real deals: **sugar, honey, molasses**. **SweetenERS** — Division 16 — are **food additives**: the substitutes. Each permitted sweetener has a table listing exactly which foods it's allowed in and at what maximum level.

**DEV:** So "agent" means real sugar, "sweetener" means additive. Naturally the similar words mean different things.

**MAYA:** Welcome to regulations. Now the cast — five to know, sorted by the key split 🎯: **digestible → low-calorie** versus **indigestible → non-caloric**.

- **Aspartame** — *digestible, low-cal*. It's literally **two amino acids** joined together, about **200× sweeter** than sucrose, so you use a trace amount. The famous caveat: people with **PKU — phenylketonuria** — can't metabolize phenylalanine, one of those amino acids, so aspartame products carry a PKU warning. Not heat-stable, by the way — it breaks down in baking.
- **Cyclamate** — *non-caloric*. The old-school one in Sweet'N Low sold in Canada.
- **Sorbitol** — *digestible, low-cal*, and it's a **sugar alcohol**. LESS sweet than sugar (~50 on the index) and famous for one party trick we'll get to.
- **Sucralose** — *non-caloric*, a whopping **600× sweeter**, and **heat-stable** — that's Splenda, the one you can actually bake with.
- **Acesulfame-K** — *non-caloric*, **200×**, also **heat-stable**; often blended with other sweeteners.

Plus the natural crowd: **stevia**, from a plant leaf, and **monk fruit** — plant-derived, intensely sweet, marketed as "natural" alternatives.

**DEV:** Okay, the sorbitol party trick.

**MAYA:** Exam radar 🎯 — a known question: which sugar substitute gives the **cool, refreshing sensation in chewing gum**? **Sorbitol.** Sugar alcohols absorb heat as they dissolve in your saliva — an endothermic effect — so your mouth literally chills. That "icy mint blast"? Half of it is thermodynamics.

**DEV:** My gum is doing physics. Unbelievable.

## Sensory science: the instrument is you

**MAYA:** Part two of the lesson: how we *perceive* food. What makes a food yummy? Three sensory factors, exam radar 🎯: **appearance, texture, and flavour — where flavour = aroma + taste.**

**DEV:** That's how everyone picks fruit, right? Apples by looking, watermelons by the thump.

**MAYA:** Exactly the lecture's examples — appearance and even *sound* are sensory data. Texture gets scientific: food scientists measure it as mechanical forces. The known question 🎯: the force needed to *squeeze* a loaf of bread is **compression**. The others in the family: cutting, **shearing**, and **tensile strength** — pulling apart.

**DEV:** So squishing bread at the store is compression testing. I'm a scientist.

**MAYA:** Unpaid, but yes. Now flavour, in two halves. **Aroma** 🎯: **FAT-soluble volatile compounds interacting with receptors in your NASAL passages.** Two keywords — fat-soluble, nose. **Taste** is the water-soluble side, on the tongue: the four classics — **sweet, sour, salty, bitter** — plus the confirmed fifth, **umami**, the savoury taste. Its ambassador: **MSG, monosodium glutamate** — a flavour enhancer that boosts savouriness and, bonus fact 🎯, **suppresses bitterness**.

**DEV:** Wait, that's why a pinch of MSG makes bitter veggies taste better?

**MAYA:** That's the mechanism. And beyond taste there are **oral sensations** — feelings, not tastes — and this matching trio is straight off a quiz 🎯:

- **Astringency** — that dry, puckery mouthfeel — caused by **tannins** (red wine, strong tea).
- **Pungency** — the burn — caused by **capsaicin** (chili peppers, spicy chips).
- **Coolness** — caused by **xylitol**, sorbitol's fellow sugar alcohol, same endothermic trick.

**DEV:** Tannins pucker, capsaicin burns, xylitol chills. Wine, hot sauce, gum.

## Sensory evaluation: three tests

**MAYA:** Last block. Sensory evaluation is a real scientific discipline for quantifying perception, and there are **three common tests** 🎯 — two known exam questions live here:

1. **Hedonic test** — asks *consumers* how much they LIKE something. Best tool for measuring **consumer acceptance of a new product**.
2. **Discriminative test**, a.k.a. **difference test** — asks: can people *tell two products apart* at all? Reformulate your cola with a new sweetener, run a difference test to see if anyone notices.
3. **Descriptive test** — a *trained panel* profiles a product on every attribute with precision. This gives the **most detail during product development**.

**DEV:** Hedonic = do you like it. Discriminative = can you tell. Descriptive = describe everything.

**MAYA:** And match the question stem: "acceptance level" → hedonic; "most details during development" → descriptive.
