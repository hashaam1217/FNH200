## The principle: it's the water activity, always

**MAYA:** Opening question 🎯: what is the principle of dehydration? Four candidates — eliminating pathogens, eliminating spoilage organisms, **removal of free water**, and **lowering of water activity**. The answer is just the last two.

**DEV:** So drying doesn't kill anything either. Same as freezing.

**MAYA:** Same lesson, different direction. Freezing *immobilizes* the free water as ice; drying *removes* it outright. Either way, **a_w** drops and microbes can't grow — but they're not dead, and neither are the pathogens. Rehydrate dried food and leave it warm, and you're back in business, microbially speaking.

**DEV:** Which is why the instructions on dried soup don't say "leave on the counter for a day."

## Water leaves from the outside in

**MAYA:** Here's the physical reality that drives everything 🎯. During dehydration, water **is removed from the surface first**, AND water **often gets trapped in the centre**. Both. The drying front moves inward, and the interior has to diffuse its water all the way out.

**DEV:** And if the surface dries too fast?

**MAYA:** You get **case hardening** — the classic exam term. The outside forms a tough, sealed shell while the inside is still wet. Now the trapped moisture can't escape, so you have a leathery crust around a damp core — unstable, prone to spoiling from within. Think of a prune that went wrong. The fix is drying gently: lower temperature, more time, so the moisture gradient stays manageable.

**DEV:** Rushing the drying ruins the drying. Very zen.

**MAYA:** And there's a cosmetic side effect worth knowing 🎯: that **white powder on dried pineapple or mango — it's sugar**, and it got there because it was **left behind on the surface as the water was removed**. It wasn't added. Water evaporates, dissolved sugar can't, so it crystallizes out on the surface. Not salt, not ascorbic acid.

## The seven drying methods

**MAYA:** Now the method list — and Judy quizzes these by matching a food to its method 🎯. Seven:

**1. Sun drying.** The oldest and — exam radar — **the most common form of dehydration worldwide**. Cheap, needs no equipment, needs good weather. Raisins, dried shiitake, dried mango.

**2. Tray (hot air) drying.** Food on trays, hot air blown over. Simple, industrial, but the heat does damage colour and flavour.

**3. Spray drying.** A liquid is atomized into a fine mist inside a hot chamber; the droplets are so tiny they dry almost instantly and fall out as powder. Exam radar 🎯: **skim milk powder, instant coffee, and other LIQUID foods** are dried by **spray drying**. If the question says liquid → powder, it's spray drying.

**4. Drum drying.** A slurry is spread in a thin film on a hot rotating drum — 120 °C steam inside — and a **"doctor blade"** scrapes off the dried sheet. The signature product: **instant mashed potato**.

**5. Vacuum microwave drying.** 🎯 **This is the one developed at UBC.** Microwaves heat the food from within while a vacuum lets water boil off at low temperature. Fast *and* gentle — you keep colour and nutrients.

**6. Deep fat frying.** Callback to Lesson 2 🎯 — the functional property of fat that matters here is that it **withstands high temperature**. The hot oil flashes the water out. Instant ramen is fried, not baked, and the exam question about it: what's the important property of deep-fat-fried instant noodles? **Rapid rehydration due to the voids left as water evaporated.** The escaping steam leaves a sponge of tiny channels, so boiling water floods back in within minutes.

**7. Extrusion drying.** Dough is forced under heat and pressure through a die; when it exits into normal pressure, the trapped steam flashes and the product **puffs**. That's Cheerios, cheese puffs, shrimp crackers, veggie straws.

**DEV:** So puffed snacks are basically controlled explosions.

**MAYA:** Delicious decompression events, yes.

## Freeze drying: the sublimation trick

**MAYA:** And now the pink strawberries. **Freeze drying** is two steps 🎯:

1. **Freeze** the food solid.
2. **Dry it under vacuum** (low pressure, low temperature) so the ice **sublimes** — goes directly from solid to vapour, skipping liquid entirely.

**DEV:** Sublimation. Same word as freeze burn from last episode.

**MAYA:** Exactly the same physics — except here we *want* it, and we control it. Now, why does this produce such a different product? Because the water leaves as vapour from wherever the ice crystals were, and each crystal leaves behind **a void**. The food never collapses. So freeze-dried food:

- **Keeps its original size and shape** — the air-dried strawberry shrivels to half the size; the freeze-dried one stays full.
- **Keeps its bright colour** 🎯 — the exam answer for those vivid pink strawberries is **low temperature drying under vacuum**. No heat means no browning, no pigment damage.
- **Rehydrates faster and better** — all those voids are open channels for water to rush back into. Between the air-dried and freeze-dried strawberry, **the freeze-dried one rehydrates better**.
- Has excellent flavour and nutrient retention.

**DEV:** Downside?

**MAYA:** It's slow and expensive — vacuum chambers and freezing aren't cheap. Which is why it's used where quality justifies cost: premium instant coffee, astronaut food, camping meals. The lecture even shows the two instant coffees side by side — the **spray-dried** one is fine, dark, and powdery; the **freeze-dried** one is larger, lighter-coloured, glassy granules.

**DEV:** I've genuinely wondered why some instant coffee is chunky and some is dust.

**MAYA:** Now you can be insufferable about it in the grocery aisle.

## Packaging: the water wants back in

**MAYA:** Last bit. You've spent all this energy removing water — so the package's job is keeping it out, plus keeping oxygen and light out too, since dried foods are high-surface-area and prone to oxidative rancidity. Exam radar 🎯: the best packaging for dried foods is **tin card / foil, because it blocks light and prevents oxidation**. That's why chip bags are metallized inside. Plastic alone is a decent water barrier but lets light through; cardboard prevents crushing but not moisture; glass looks nice and does nothing useful here.

**DEV:** So the shiny inside of a chip bag is doing real work.

**MAYA:** It's a light shield, an oxygen barrier, and a moisture barrier in one. The bag is the most engineered part of the chips.
