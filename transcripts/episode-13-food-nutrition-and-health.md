## Three categories, one confusing aisle

**MAYA:** Start with who invented this space 🎯: **which country led the way in regulating functional foods?** **Japan** — with a system called **FOSHU: Food for Specific Health Use.** Not Canada, not the US.

**DEV:** Japan got there first.

**MAYA:** By years. Canada's parallel framework is **Natural and Non-Prescription Health Products**. Now the three categories, and the exam tests them by showing you a product 🎯:

- **Functional food** — a **food** that provides a health benefit beyond basic nutrition. Shown as: **orange juice with added calcium and vitamin D** → **functional food**. It's still juice; you consume it as food.
- **Natural health product (NHP)** — a **pill, capsule, or supplement** form. Shown as: **calcium chewable tablets** → **natural health product**, *not* a functional food. The dosage form is the tell.
- **Nutraceutical** — the isolated bioactive compound itself.

**DEV:** So the same calcium is a functional food in juice and a natural health product in a tablet.

**MAYA:** The molecule doesn't decide the category — **the format does**. That's exactly what those two slides are testing.

## What counts as a natural health product

**MAYA:** Under the **Natural Health Products Regulations, in effect January 1, 2004** 🎯, NHPs include — and this is an emphatic **all of the above**:

**vitamins and minerals · herbal remedies · homeopathic medicines · traditional medicines** (such as traditional Chinese medicines) · **probiotics** · and other products like **amino acids and essential fatty acids**.

NHPs must be **safe as over-the-counter products and need no prescription**. Anything requiring a prescription is regulated as a **drug** under the Food and Drug Regulations instead.

**DEV:** So how do I check the bottles in my cabinet?

**MAYA:** Best practical question in the course 🎯. How do you know a product is authorized for sale in Canada? Not because the label says "Canadian." Not because the manufacturer is old and reputable. Not because it makes a health claim. **Because it has an NPN — Natural Product Number — or a DIN-HM (Homeopathic Medicine Number).**

Those numbers are searchable in the **Licensed Natural Health Product Database**, which lists product name, licence holder, NPN/DIN-HM, medicinal and non-medicinal ingredients, dosage form, **recommended use or purpose** (the authorized health claim), and **risk information** — cautions, warnings, contraindications, known adverse reactions.

**DEV:** So if there's no NPN on the bottle, Health Canada hasn't authorized it.

**MAYA:** That's the entire consumer takeaway of Lesson 13. Go look at your creatine tub tonight.

## Making a functional food

**MAYA:** Four ways to develop one 🎯:

1. **Adding vitamins and/or minerals**
2. **Adding bioactive ingredients**
3. Producing foods with **increased levels of bioactives through breeding and/or genetic modification**
4. Producing foods with increased bioactives **through processing and/or livestock feeding**

**DEV:** Number four — livestock feeding?

**MAYA:** Omega-3 eggs. You feed the hens flaxseed, and the omega-3 ends up in the yolk. You've engineered a functional food without touching the egg.

Examples from the slides 🎯 — the question shows Benecol cholesterol-lowering spread, oat bran powder with beta-glucan, green tea with ginseng, probiotic yogurt, **and plain carrots** — and the answer is **all of them**. Carrots count because functional foods include foods whose bioactives (beta-carotene) are simply *naturally there*. You don't have to add anything.

**DEV:** Whole vegetables were functional foods before marketing existed.

**MAYA:** And Canadian examples worth a nod: **Praeventia** cookies with red wine extract, **haskap** berry products from the Maritimes, dried apple chips, **Milk2Go Sport** protein shakes. On the NHP side: the **Pacific Agri-Food Research Centre** extracting **anthocyanins** from berries and red wine, **Nutra Canada** (fruit and vegetable extracts), **Greenfield Naturals** (protein and fibre from corn-ethanol by-products), **Nanton Nutraceuticals** (Rhoziva from *Rhodiola rosea*), **Island Abbey Foods** (Honibe honey lozenges), **Natural Factors** (PGX for appetite control), and **Cold-FX from North American ginseng**.

**DEV:** Corn-ethanol by-products becoming protein and fibre. The waste-to-value thing again.

**MAYA:** Fifth time this course. It's practically the industry's business model.

## Probiotics: the numbers matter

**MAYA:** Last content block. **Known benefits of probiotics** 🎯: **reduce gastrointestinal discomfort and disorder, control irritable bowel syndrome, and prevent development of colon cancer** — with many more potential benefits still under investigation. The colon cancer mechanism is thought to involve metabolic conversion, degradation, or absorption of carcinogenic compounds, plus immune stimulation.

Critically, the bacteria must be **viable — alive** — when you eat them, and survive distribution, retail, and your fridge.

**DEV:** And the named organisms?

**MAYA:** ***Lactobacillus acidophilus*** and ***Bifidobacterium bifidus*** — real, recognized probiotic species. Which sets up my favourite question of the lesson 🎯: **is *Bifidus regularis* an approved health-promoting probiotic added to yogurt?**

**DEV:** It's on the Activia container in big letters, so… yes?

**MAYA:** **No.** *"Bifidus regularis"* is a **trademarked marketing name**, not an approved probiotic designation. Same style of trick as "Canadian" on the ginseng label — branding that looks like regulatory language and isn't.

**DEV:** Brutal. And the dose?

**MAYA:** 🎯 **1.0 × 10⁹ units of approved probiotics must be present in each serving** for a product to make a probiotic claim. For actual health benefit, the slide gives **1.0 × 10⁹ units per mL, and 100 mL twice a week**. Note the Canvas lesson text words the threshold as **above 10⁶ (1 million) viable organisms per mL**, with the same "100 mL twice per week" — if a question asks for the *claim* threshold, go with **10⁹ per serving**.

**DEV:** A billion bacteria per serving. Per *serving*.

**MAYA:** And they have to still be alive when they reach you. That's the hard part of the business.

## Two sample short answers (with the marking scheme)

**MAYA:** Judy showed two full worked examples. These are gold.

**Question 1 — the green potatoes.** You're making potato salad for your grandpa's birthday, you get home, the potatoes have turned green, and you have an hour and no money for alternatives.

**(a) What's the matter, and why is it a concern? (2 marks)** Green potatoes contain higher-than-normal **solanine** — **80 to 100 mg per 100 g**, versus a normal few mg per 100 g. Solanine is a **cholinesterase inhibitor** and can **affect nerve function** at an oral dose of about **100 mg**.

**(b) Will trimming the green parts help? (2 marks)** Yes — because **solanine cannot be destroyed by heat**, and it **concentrates in the green portion**, so **trimming the green part is an effective way to lower the hazard**.

**(c) What would you do? (1 mark)** Judy's answer: she'd **not bring the potato salad** — grandpa is elderly with a weaker immune system and will eat a lot of it. And the marking note in bold: **you must DECLARE a position** — yes or no — *then* explain, to earn the mark.

**DEV:** So the failure mode is writing a thoughtful "on the one hand…" with no actual decision.

**MAYA:** Zero marks for fence-sitting. Pick a side, justify it.

**Question 2 — camping pasta.** A camping supply company is developing quality pasta in tomato sauce for multi-day hikers. **What preservation technique (3 points)? What packaging (2 points)?**

**Best answer:** **Dehydration — freeze drying or vacuum microwave drying.** Why: the **vacuum removes water at low temperature**, **preventing quality loss**; the dried pasta is **lighter to carry** for a multi-day hike; and freeze-dried products **rehydrate easily** thanks to the **void structure** created during drying. Packaging: a **strong water barrier such as plastic**, and **lightweight** for the hiker.

**Partial-credit answer:** **Commercial sterilization** — eliminates all possible pathogens, but the **high temperature affects quality**; still easy to consume in the wilderness. Packaging: a **retort pouch pre-sterilized with hydrogen peroxide**, or pasta and sauce sealed in a pouch then heated — **lightweight** for hiking.

**Explicitly NOT acceptable:** **pasteurization, freezing, irradiation, cans, or glass.**

**DEV:** Cans and glass because a hiker isn't carrying those up a mountain. Freezing because there's no freezer on a trail. Pasteurization because it isn't shelf-stable. Irradiation because — pasta isn't on Canada's approved list.

**MAYA:** And notice what the marking scheme rewards: **name the method, explain the mechanism, then give reasons tied to the actual scenario.** Weight matters because it's hiking. Rehydration matters because they'll add water on a trail. Answer the situation, not the topic.
