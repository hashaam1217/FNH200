## The Food and Drugs Act: four sections to know

**MAYA:** The foundation is the **Food and Drugs Act** — an Act respecting food, drugs, cosmetics and therapeutic devices. Four sections carry the exam weight 🎯, and each has a keyword:

- **Section 3 — no miracle cures.** You cannot advertise any food, drug, or cosmetic to the general public as a **treatment, preventative, or cure** for the serious diseases on Schedule A — a list that includes cancer, diabetes, depression, heart disease, hypertension, obesity, arthritis, and more. That's why granola can't claim to cure anything.
- **Section 4 — no selling gross food.** No person shall **sell** food that contains poisonous or harmful substances, is unfit for human consumption, is — actual legal language — "filthy, putrid, disgusting, rotten, decomposed," is adulterated, or was made under unsanitary conditions.
- **Section 5 — no lying.** No labelling, packaging, processing, or advertising that is **false, misleading, or deceptive** about a food's character, value, quantity, composition, merit, or safety.
- **Section 7 — no dirty kitchens.** No person shall **manufacture, prepare, preserve, package, or store** food for sale **under unsanitary conditions**.

**DEV:** So: 3 no cures, 4 no gross sales, 5 no lies, 7 no dirty factories.

**MAYA:** That's the mnemonic. Notice 4 is about the *food itself* being sold; 7 is about the *conditions* it's made in.

## Two agencies, one handshake

**MAYA:** Guaranteed exam question 🎯: who runs this system? Two players.

**Health Canada** — specifically the **Health Products and Food Branch, HPFB** — **administers** the Food and Drugs Act. They *write the rules*: standards of identity and composition, and the food additive regulations. When an additive like aspartame or nitrite needs approval, **Health Canada** is the approver.

**The Canadian Food Inspection Agency — CFIA** — does **enforcement**: inspections, compliance, and **food labelling** for industry. If a label is inaccurate, that's CFIA's desk.

**DEV:** Health Canada writes the law, CFIA enforces it. Brains and muscle.

**MAYA:** And the two known questions map exactly: "administration vs enforcement" → Health Canada HPFB, then CFIA. "Which agency is responsible for accurate labelling?" → **CFIA**. "Who approves additives?" → **Health Canada**. Don't let them swap.

## Anatomy of a label

**MAYA:** Grab anything from your pantry. By law, six things must be on it 🎯:

1. **Bilingual** — English and French
2. **Common name** of the food
3. **Net quantity**
4. **Name of the business**
5. **Ingredient list**
6. **Nutrition Facts table**

— all legible, with placement rules.

**DEV:** And the extra stuff — best-before dates, "may contain nuts"?

**MAYA:** That's the second tier: required *depending on the food*. **Best before date. Country of origin. Sweetener declarations** — aspartame products must carry that phenylalanine warning from last episode. **Irradiated foods** get a symbol — wait for Lesson 10. **Percent milk fat** on dairy, **percent alcohol** on booze. **Allergens.**

The known trick question 🎯: which is NOT mandatory on a *yogurt* label? Net quantity — mandatory. % milk fat — mandatory, it's dairy. Nutrition Facts — mandatory. Ingredients — mandatory. **% alcohol — not mandatory**, because yogurt is not, typically, booze.

**DEV:** "Typically."

**MAYA:** Kombucha walks a fine line; yogurt behaves. Two more label features. **Disease risk reduction claims** — remember Section 3 bans cure claims, but there's a short CFIA-approved list of risk *reduction* claims, and exam radar 🎯, there are exactly **five**: sodium/potassium and blood pressure; calcium/vitamin D and osteoporosis; saturated and trans fat and heart disease; vegetables and fruit and cancer risk; and sugar-free gum and dental caries. Five. Count them on one hand.

And the newest arrival: the **front-of-package label** — Health Canada's black-and-white **magnifying glass** symbol flagging foods **high in saturated fat, sugars, or sodium**. That's rolling onto pop, candy bars, salty snacks — the government literally putting a warning spotlight on the front of the box.

## Standardized foods and the grade ladder

**MAYA:** Back to the ice cream scandal. Many foods in Canada are **standardized foods**: the Food and Drug Regulations prescribe **what must be in them and how much** — a standard of identity. Bread, enriched white bread, raisin bread, whole wheat bread: all standardized. Ice cream: standardized, minimum milk fat required — hence "frozen dessert" for the fatless impostor. Something like pizza dough? Not standardized — no legal recipe exists.

**DEV:** So a standardized food is one with a legal recipe. Deviate and you have to rename it.

**MAYA:** Precisely. Then, on top of identity, comes **quality grading** 🎯 — and grade names are prime multiple-choice bait:

- **Eggs: Canada Grade A, Grade B, Grade C, and Nest Run.** The distractor answers will offer "Canada Fancy" for eggs — refuse.
- **Processed fruit** (like canned peaches): **Canada Fancy, Canada Choice, Canada Standard.** Fun classroom demo: the no-name "Fancy" peaches can out-grade the big brand's "Choice" — grade and brand prestige are independent. Read the grade, not the logo.
- **Beef: Canada A, AA, AAA, Prime** — climbing the ladder of **marbling**, the white flecks of fat within the muscle. Canada A requires just *some* visible marbling; Prime is the most marbled.

**DEV:** So eggs get letters, peaches get adjectives, beef gets A's. Naturally, no system agrees with any other.

## Food additives: Canada's short list

**MAYA:** Now the additive chapter. First, the number that always gets tested 🎯: Canada has roughly **300 approved food additives**. The United States? **Over 2,600.**

**DEV:** Ten times?! Is American food ten times more additive-y?

**MAYA:** Mostly no — it's a *definition* difference. Canada's definition of "food additive" **excludes** things the US counts: **nutritive materials, seasonings, agricultural residues, food packaging materials, and veterinary drugs**. Same molecules, different filing cabinets. Canada's ~300 real additives live in **Division 16** of the regulations, in tables listing each additive, exactly **which foods it's permitted in**, and its **maximum level of use**. An additive isn't "approved" in general — it's approved for *specific* uses at *specific* levels.

**DEV:** Okay, and how does something get onto that list?

**MAYA:** A brutal Health Canada dossier 🎯: the additive's composition, properties and method of manufacture; the amount and purpose of use; a method of analysis; data on its intended technical effect; **detailed reporting of safety tests** — research, research, research; a proposed maximum residue limit; label specimens; and an actual sample.

And the safety logic is the best concept in this lesson: the **dose-response curve**. Low doses: flat line, **no effect**. Then a **threshold**, then rising response, then maximum effect. Two acronyms 🎯:

- **ADI — Acceptable Daily Intake**: derived from the highest *no-effect* level, with safety margins. The amount you could eat daily for life, safely.
- **PDI — Probable Daily Intake**: what people will *actually* consume based on real diets.

The approval rule: **PDI must be far below ADI**. And the known question 🎯: what if the PDI *exceeds* the ADI? The additive will **not be approved — or approved only with restricted use**. Both. It's the "B or C" answer.

**DEV:** Real numbers?

**MAYA:** Aspartame. A can of diet pop has about **200 mg**; you'd need around **twelve cans a day** to even reach the ADI. On the dose-response graph, your actual intake sits deep in the no-effect flatline. Also from the lecture: aspartame digests into **aspartic acid, phenylalanine, and methanol** — scary-sounding, but the amounts are tiny, and the genuine concern remains PKU, as covered last episode. Oh — and "the dose makes the poison" cuts both ways: even common foods have a dose-response curve; even water has a toxic dose. Poisons just have a much, much lower threshold.

## Nitrites: the additive with a story arc

**MAYA:** The lesson's grand finale: **nitrites in cured meats** — ham, bacon, deli slices. Why add them? Exam radar 🎯 — it's an "all of the above":

1. **Colour.** Chemistry time: heat meat normally and red **myoglobin** turns into brown **metmyoglobin** — cooked-beef grey-brown. But react myoglobin with **nitrite** and you get **nitrosylhemochrome** — the stable PINK of ham. That's why ham is pink and roast beef is brown. Same starting pigment, different reaction.
2. **Flavour.** Meat + nitrite + salt + heat = that signature cured-meat flavour.
3. **Antimicrobial.** The big one: nitrite blocks the spores of ***Clostridium botulinum*** — the botulism organism — from germinating into toxin-producing cells. Vacuum-packed, low-oxygen deli meat is exactly where botulism would thrive. Nitrite is the guard.

**DEV:** And the catch? There's clearly a catch.

**MAYA:** The catch 🎯, and the precise wording matters: **nitrite + amines + heat → nitrosamines**, and **NITROSAMINES are the potentially carcinogenic compound.** Not nitrates, not nitrites themselves, not nitrosylhemochrome — the exam *will* line all of those up as options. The carcinogen is the downstream reaction product, formed under high heat — think well-crisped bacon.

**DEV:** So why is nitrite still legal?

**MAYA:** The known three-part answer — all of the above: **foods are not the major source of nitrosamine exposure; the risk appears very low; and no suitable replacement has been found to control *C. botulinum*.** It's risk-benefit arithmetic: a small, managed carcinogen risk versus botulism, which can kill you this week. Regulators cap nitrite levels, industry adds inhibitors, and the pink stays.

**DEV:** So my ham is a negotiated settlement.

**MAYA:** Every label is. That's honestly the theme of the lesson.
