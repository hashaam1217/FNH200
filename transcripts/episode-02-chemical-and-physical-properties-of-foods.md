## Colloidal dispersions: everything is tiny bits in other stuff

**MAYA:** Big idea number one. Most foods aren't simple solutions — they're **colloidal dispersions**: particles of one substance distributed in another **without dissolving**. Two vocabulary words, exam radar 🎯: the stuff that's scattered around inside is the **dispersed phase**; the stuff that surrounds it — the exterior, the outside — is the **continuous phase**.

**DEV:** So chocolate chips in a cookie. Chips dispersed, cookie continuous.

**MAYA:** Spiritually correct. Now, Table 2.1 — this is *the* table of Lesson 2, and it's a matching-question goldmine. Six dispersions:

- **Solid in liquid → sol.** Starches and proteins in water. A sol helps hold small pieces of food suspended in liquid — think the body of a salad dressing.
- **Liquid in liquid → emulsion.** Milk, mayonnaise.
- **Liquid in solid → solid emulsion.** Butter, margarine.
- **Liquid in solid network → gel.** Starch, pectin, or gelatin gels. Jello. Tofu.
- **Gas in liquid → foam.** Beaten egg white, whipped frosting.
- **Gas in solid → solid foam.** Meringue, ice cream, bread.

**DEV:** Wait, ice cream is a *solid foam*? Like a sponge?

**MAYA:** Ice cream is basically frozen air scaffolding — it's whipped full of gas, and by regulation it must be at least 36% solids. It's honestly several dispersions at once: an emulsion *and* a solid foam. Angel cake and marshmallows, same family — exam radar 🎯, that's a known question: angel cake or marshmallow = **solid foam**.

**DEV:** And milk?

**MAYA:** Careful — this is a precision trap. "Milk is an emulsion" is true, but the *most accurate* answer is: **milk is an oil-in-water emulsion**. Fat droplets dispersed in a watery continuous phase. Flip it around and you get **butter and margarine: water-in-oil** — water droplets trapped in continuous fat. On the exam, always ask "which phase is on the outside?"

**DEV:** Okay, but if milk is oil droplets in water… why doesn't the fat float to the top like my sad peanut butter?

**MAYA:** *Chef's kiss* — that's the exact question the lecture asks. In fresh-from-the-cow milk it *does* — that's cream rising. In commercial milk, two things happen: homogenization smashes the fat into tiny droplets, and **milk proteins coat each droplet as a natural emulsifier**, keeping them suspended. Your peanut butter has no emulsifier and no stabilizer, so gravity wins and oil creams out — well, the reverse: the oil floats up.

**DEV:** So what's the difference between an emulsifier and a stabilizer?

**MAYA:** Exam radar 🎯. An **emulsifier** sits at the oil–water interface holding droplets in the mix; a **stabilizer** thickens the continuous phase so droplets can't migrate and float to the top — that floating is called *creaming*. Salad dressings add stabilizers for exactly this. And the superstar emulsifier: **phospholipids** — special fats that are partly water-loving and partly fat-loving. Hydrophilic head in the water, hydrophobic tail in the oil. The famous one is **lecithin**, found in egg yolk and soybeans — it's in everything from mayo to chocolate.

**DEV:** That's why mayo needs egg.

**MAYA:** Exactly. Egg yolk lecithin holds oil and vinegar together. No lecithin, no mayo — just regret and separation.

## Carbohydrates: a sweetness power ranking

**MAYA:** On to the major components. Carbs first — meet the family. **Monosaccharides**, single units: glucose, fructose, galactose. **Disaccharides**, pairs — and exam radar 🎯, know the couples *and* the enzymes that split them:

- **Sucrose** = glucose + fructose. Table sugar. Digested by **invertase**.
- **Lactose** = glucose + galactose. Milk sugar. Digested by **lactase**.
- **Maltose** = glucose + glucose. Digested by **maltase**.

And **polysaccharides**: very long chains of glucose — starch.

**DEV:** Enzyme names all end in -ase and match the sugar. Even I can hold that.

**MAYA:** Now the sweetness index, Table 2.2. Sucrose is the benchmark at **100**. Above it: **fructose, 140** — the sweetest natural sugar. **Invert sugar, 100–130.** At par: xylitol, 100. Below: **glucose 70–80**, galactose 60, corn syrup and sorbitol 50, **maltose 20**, and **lactose scraping the bottom at 10–20**.

**DEV:** Milk sugar is barely sweet at all?

**MAYA:** Which sets up the best magic trick in this lesson. **Lactose-free milk tastes sweeter than regular milk.** Same calories, same carbs. Why?

**DEV:** Because… they added lactase, which chops lactose into glucose and galactose… and those are *both* sweeter than lactose was?

**MAYA:** Ten points. Lactose is a 10–20; its two halves are a 70–80 and a 60. Splitting the molecule literally raises the sweetness. Same trick with **invert sugar**: hit sucrose with invertase or acid, get glucose + fructose, and the mix is sweeter than the sucrose you started with.

**DEV:** And high fructose corn syrup?

**MAYA:** Same logic, industrial scale — exam radar 🎯 for the pathway: **starch** (not sweet) → *amylase or acid* → **maltose** (slightly sweet) → *maltase* → **glucose** (sweet) → *glucose isomerase* → **fructose** (very sweet). HFCS comes in grades: 42% fructose ≈ 100, 55% is 100+, 90% hits 120–160. One caution from the lecture: a ranking question with generic "corn syrup" in it was a deliberate trick — plain corn syrup is a variable glucose–maltose mixture, so *there wasn't enough info to rank it*. Read sweetness questions carefully.

**DEV:** Sneaky. Okay — sugar's other jobs? The jam question?

**MAYA:** Exam radar 🎯: besides sweetening, the primary role of sugar in jam is **preservative**. Sugar binds water, which — hold that thought — starves microbes. That's also foreshadowing the water section.

## Browning: Maillard vs caramelization

**MAYA:** Two browning reactions, one distinction, endless exam questions. **Maillard reaction: sugar + protein** — technically a reducing sugar plus an amino compound. Bread crust, toast, roasted coffee, seared meat. **Caramelization: sugar ONLY** — caramel, toffee.

**DEV:** So the trick question is something browned that has no protein?

**MAYA:** Or the reverse — the known question is "which is NOT a Maillard product?" with baked bread, roasted coffee, toast… and **sugar cane**. Sugar cane is just sugar — nothing's even been browned. If both sugar and protein are present and it's browning under heat, think Maillard. Sugar alone in a hot pan, think caramelization.

**DEV:** Toast = Maillard. Toffee = caramelization. Locked in.

## Starch's life cycle, and the gravel rice

**MAYA:** Your rice question. Three terms, exam radar 🎯. When you cook rice or pasta, starch granules absorb water and swell — that's **gelatinization**. When the leftovers sit in the fridge and harden into gravel, the starch molecules recrystallize — that's **retrogradation**. And when a gel weeps liquid — water squeezed out of old yogurt or jelly — that's **syneresis**.

**DEV:** Cooking is gelatinization, going stale is retrogradation, weeping is syneresis. So stale bread isn't *dried out*?

**MAYA:** Mostly retrogradation, yes — which is why a splash of water and a hot oven partially re-gelatinizes it and revives the loaf. Food science: occasionally useful at brunch. There's a fourth term, **dextrins** — smaller starch fragments — parked until Lesson 4.

**MAYA:** Also filed under carbs — the thickener all-stars. Judy explicitly cares about the **sources**, so exam radar 🎯 on this list: **pectin** — from plants like apple, the cement between plant cells; gelling agent in jams, viscosity in ketchup and tomato paste. **Agar** — from seaweed (kelp), thickener. **Alginates** — from seaweed, gelling agents, keep solids suspended in fruit juices. **Gum arabic** — from the **bark of the acacia tree**, thickener and stabilizer. **Carrageenan** — from **red algae** seaweed; it's what keeps cocoa particles floating in chocolate milk. **Xanthan gum** — made by **bacteria**, first isolated from rotting cabbage, thickens salad dressings.

**DEV:** "First isolated from rotting cabbage" is the least appetizing origin story ever put on a nutrition label.

## Fats and proteins: the multi-tools

**MAYA:** Rapid tour of the other two majors. In food *science* — as opposed to nutrition — we talk about fat's **functional roles**, and there are seven on the list 🎯: **aerate, carry aroma, emulsify, lubricate, soften, tenderize, withstand high temperature.** The known question: why do we deep-fry french fries in oil? Because oil can **withstand high temperature** — way hotter than boiling water, hence the crispy crust.

**DEV:** And "carry aroma" is why butter-fried anything smells illegal?

**MAYA:** Flavour compounds dissolve in fat, yes. Proteins, meanwhile, have two structural superpowers: they **foam** and they **gel**. Whipped egg white → foam → bake it → meringue, a solid foam. Bread's airy crumb — protein-stabilized foam. The latte question 🎯: what makes the foam on your latte? **Milk protein.** Not the fat, not the lactose — protein is the foamer. And gels: cooked egg white, sausages, tofu — proteins unfolding and setting into a network.

**DEV:** Proteins have a dark side though, right?

**MAYA:** The unwelcome role: **allergens**. Food allergens are proteins, and Health Canada and the CFIA maintain Canada's priority allergen list — eggs, milk, mustard, peanuts, crustaceans and molluscs, fish, sesame, soy, sulphites, tree nuts, and wheat. That's why labels shout "CONTAINS: PEANUTS."

## Water: the most important ingredient you ignore

**MAYA:** Now — the lecture asks, what's the *most important* component in food? Answer: **water**. And here's the concept that separates FNH 200 students from civilians: **moisture content is not water activity.**

**DEV:** Those sound identical.

**MAYA:** Everyone thinks so, which is why it's a killer exam question 🎯. There are two kinds of water in food. **Bound water** is stuck to food components — salt, sugar, fat, protein — and isn't available for anything. **Free water** is the dangerous kind: available for **microbial growth, chemical reactions, and enzymatic reactions**. **Water activity, a_w**, measures the *free* water — scale of 0 to 1, pure water = 1.0. It's defined by vapour pressure at equilibrium, and there are lab instruments that measure it.

**DEV:** So two foods can have the same moisture but different water activity?

**MAYA:** The lecture proves it. Rank these by water activity: apple sauce at 95% moisture, fresh egg 75%, fresh meat 72%, apple jelly 35%. Trick question — **you can't, from moisture alone.** Jelly is loaded with sugar binding its water, so its a_w is far lower than its moisture implies. That's *why* sugar preserves jam and salt preserves fish: they convert free water into bound water. Microbes are surrounded by moisture they can't drink.

**DEV:** Hostile hydration environment.

**MAYA:** And roughly: most **bacteria need a_w above ~0.91**, most **yeasts ~0.88**, most **molds ~0.80** — with specialist osmophilic yeasts and xerophilic molds surviving down around 0.62–0.65. Drop water activity low enough and nothing grows. This number comes roaring back in the dehydration lesson.

## The minor components (that are majorly testable)

**MAYA:** Last section — minor constituents: acids, pigments, aroma compounds, vitamins, minerals. Small amounts, big consequences.

**Acids.** Every fruit has a signature acid, and exam radar 🎯: the major acid in **apple is MALIC acid**. Citric belongs to citrus, tartaric to grapes, lactic to fermented dairy. And the single most important number in this course: **pH 4.6**. A food with pH below 4.6 is high-acid; above is low-acid. The lecture literally made a joke slide — "which pH is most critical? A. 4.6, B. 4.6, C. 4.6, D. 4.6, E. 4.6."

**DEV:** Subtle. Why 4.6 though?

**MAYA:** Full story in Lesson 6, but the preview: below pH 4.6, the botulism bacterium can't do its thing. That number decides how aggressively a canned food must be processed. Tattoo it somewhere.

**MAYA:** **Pigments** — why cooked lobster turns red, why cured ham stays pink, why ground beef goes brown in the pan: pigment chemistry reacting to heat and curing. **Aroma constituents** — coffee alone has a whole wheel of aroma descriptors and a parade of aromatic compounds; aroma is chemistry you can smell. **Vitamins** moonlight as additives 🎯: **vitamin C (ascorbic acid)** works as a *bleaching agent* in flour whitening and as a *preservative/antioxidant* slowing rancidity and browning; **vitamin E (tocopherols)** is also an antioxidant preservative. And **minerals**: the calcium in tofu isn't just nutrition — **calcium sulphate is the coagulant** that sets soy milk into a gel. The mineral literally builds the food.

**DEV:** So tofu is a calcium-set protein gel, which makes it… a colloidal dispersion. We've gone full circle.
