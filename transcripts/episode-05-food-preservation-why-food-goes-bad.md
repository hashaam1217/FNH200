## Microbes: the good, the bad, and the ugly

**MAYA:** Star villains first. Microorganisms come in a power ranking, and this is a known question 🎯 — which is *most resistant* in a normal environment?

- **Vegetative bacterial cells** — the ordinary, active, living bacteria. Most common, easiest to kill.
- **Yeasts** — hardier, survive less-ideal environments; famous for fermentation.
- **Moulds** — tolerate a *wide* range of pH and water activity; they'll grow where nothing else can.
- **Bacterial SPORES** — the winners. Armored survival capsules that shrug off heat, drought, and disinfectant. The infamous example: ***Clostridium botulinum*** spores — our nemesis from the nitrite story, and the main character of next episode.

**DEV:** So spores are bacteria in bunker mode.

**MAYA:** Exactly — dormant, nearly indestructible, waiting for conditions to improve. Which sets up sorting microbes by *what conditions they need*. Two classification systems, both exam bait:

**By oxygen** 🎯: **aerobic** (need oxygen), **anaerobic** (grow without it — oxygen can even poison them), and **facultative anaerobes** (flexible — either way). The killer application question: what can grow inside a *sealed can*, where there's no oxygen? **Anaerobes and facultative anaerobes.** Not aerobes. This is exactly why canned food's threat is *C. botulinum* — an anaerobe that loves the oxygen-free penthouse of a sealed can.

**By temperature** 🎯 — four classes, with growth ranges:

- **Psychrophiles**: 0–18 °C — cold specialists
- **Psychrotrophs**: **−5 to 35 °C** — cold-*tolerant* generalists
- **Mesophiles**: 10–45 °C — the middle crowd, human-body-temperature fans
- **Thermophiles**: 50 to over 100 °C — heat freaks

The known question: who spoils **refrigerated food**? Gut instinct says psychrophiles — "psychro" means cold, right? Wrong 🎯 — it's the **psychrotrophs**. Their huge range means they grow fine at fridge temperature *and* fast at room temperature, so they dominate. That's the slow slime on refrigerated deli meat.

**DEV:** Sneaky. The trick answer is the one that *sounds* colder.

**MAYA:** Precisely why it's a good exam question. Now — the lecture's Western: microbes are **Good, Bad, and Ugly** 🎯.

- **Good = fermentation.** And which organisms can ferment? Vegetative bacteria — salami, kimchi. Yeasts — beer, bread. Moulds — brie's white rind, miso. So bacteria, yeasts, AND moulds — but *not* bacterial spores. Spores don't ferment anything; they just lurk.
- **Bad = spoilage.** They ruin food — slime, souring, off-odours — but don't necessarily hurt you.
- **Ugly = foodborne illness.** They make you sick — and here's the scary part, they often *don't* change the taste or smell at all.

**DEV:** So the mould on brie is Good, the mould on my bread is Bad, and the invisible stuff that sends you to the ER is Ugly. Same category of organism, three career paths.

**MAYA:** Moulds are the perfect example — the lecture shows brie and mouldy bread side by side. Context is everything.

## Enzymes: the inside job

**MAYA:** Now your apple. That browning wasn't microbes — the apple did it to *itself*. Cutting the apple ruptured cells and let an enzyme meet oxygen. Exam radar 🎯, the name: **polyphenol oxidase**. It oxidizes phenolic compounds into brown pigments. This is **enzymatic browning** — a third browning pathway, completely separate from Maillard and caramelization in Lesson 2.

**DEV:** So browning has three flavours: sugar+protein Maillard, sugar-only caramelization, and enzyme-plus-oxygen for cut fruit.

**MAYA:** Beautifully filed. And the counterattack question is a classic "all of the above" 🎯 — how do you stop apple slices browning?

- **Dip in hot water** — blanching denatures the enzyme. Dead enzyme, no browning.
- **Dip in plain water** — blocks oxygen from reaching the surface.
- **Water with lemon juice** — acid drops the pH below where the enzyme works.
- **Sulfur dioxide** — the industrial inhibitor, why dried apples stay pale.

**All of the above.** Notice each one attacks a different requirement: the enzyme, the oxygen, the pH. That's preservation thinking in miniature.

**DEV:** Kill the worker, cut the supplies, or ruin the office.

**MAYA:** One more apple mystery 🎯: the lecture shows an apple browned on the INSIDE — never cut, brown core. Cause? **Improper storage temperature.** Internal browning is storage injury — remember those controlled-atmosphere rooms from Episode 1; get the conditions wrong and the flesh dies from within. Not sunlight, not parasites — temperature.

## Chemistry, physics, and stale bread — again

**MAYA:** The non-living villains, rapid fire. **Oxygen**: fats + oxygen = **oxidation and lipolytic rancidity** — that stale-paint smell in old chips and nuts. **Physical damage**: crushed berries, pulverized cookies — broken cells spoil faster and nobody buys crumbs. **Moisture migration**: soggy cereal, rock-hard brown sugar.

And a Lesson 2 callback with a twist 🎯 — why does bread go tough and dry? **Retrogradation** of starch, and the exam version is an all-of-the-above: **syneresis squeezing water out from between starch molecules, moisture loss during storage, AND exclusion of the water absorbed during gelatinization.** All three descriptions of the same sad process.

**DEV:** Stale bread: still the most over-explained tragedy in food science.

## Perishability: the three-tier fridge

**MAYA:** Last framework 🎯 — foods sort by how fast they die:

- **Perishable** — days: fresh meat, fish, milk, soft produce.
- **Semi-perishable** — weeks to months: and the known question lists **ice cream, cheese, and potato chips**. They last a while but *are* slowly deteriorating — ice crystals grow, chips go rancid.
- **Shelf-stable** — months to years: canned goods, sugar, flour, and **dehydrated pasta** — that's the odd one *out* in the exam question. Pasta is shelf-stable, not semi-perishable.

**DEV:** Chips are "semi-perishable"? They've survived in my car for a semester.

**MAYA:** *Survived* is doing heavy lifting there — oxidative rancidity has absolutely visited your car. And with the enemies mapped, the goals of preservation are the course's oldest trio — keep food **safe, nutritious, and quality** — knowing we can only ever delay the inevitable.
